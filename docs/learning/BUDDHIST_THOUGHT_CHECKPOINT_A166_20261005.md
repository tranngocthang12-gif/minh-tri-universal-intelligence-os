## Central question

How does early Buddhist material allow forceful correction of what is false or harmful while preventing disagreement from becoming hatred, contempt, factional identity, or status competition?

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. MN 58 — truth, benefit, timing, and compassion govern correction

MN 58 does not say that a true statement should always be uttered immediately or harshly.

The discourse distinguishes speech by:
- truth/falsity;
- benefit/harm;
- pleasant/unpleasant reception;
- suitable timing.

The Buddha may say something unwelcome if it is true and beneficial, but timing and compassionate concern still matter.

Therefore:
**truthfulness does not require aggression.**

### 2. MN 21 — non-hatred is required even under extreme provocation

MN 21 gives very strong training against hatred and ill will, including the famous severe-provocation similes.

Its relevance for disagreement is bounded but clear:
the presence of wrong or harmful conduct in another does not license cultivating hatred.

Thus:
**opposition to an act/view and hatred toward a person are not the same mental event.**

### 3. AN 5.198 — admonition has conditions

AN 5.198 gives conditions for admonishing another, including:
- speaking at the right time;
- truthfully;
- gently;
- beneficially;
- with a mind of goodwill.

This is direct evidence that correction itself is disciplined.

The goal is not silence.
The goal is correction without unwholesome motive.

### 4. MN 103 — roots of dispute include inner faults, not only doctrinal difference

MN 103 analyzes causes of disputes among practitioners, including anger, hostility, contempt, envy, hypocrisy, obstinacy, attachment to one's own view, and unwillingness to relinquish.

This shifts diagnosis from:
"we disagree because one proposition differs"

to:
"disagreement can become conflict because defilements organize the disagreement."

### 5. MN 104 — concord requires procedures, not sentimental agreement

MN 104 addresses communal harmony and methods for settling disputes.

It shows that harmony is not equivalent to pretending there is no disagreement.

A disciplined community can:
- identify issues;
- use procedures;
- distinguish offense/non-offense;
- settle disputes;
- preserve concord.

Therefore:
**non-enmity does not mean avoiding correction or process.**

### 6. Snp 4.8 / 4.12 — debate becomes identity conflict when truth is possessed as status

Aṭṭhakavagga repeatedly shows how people:
- hold "my doctrine";
- claim exclusive truth;
- seek victory;
- despise opponents;
- become elated in winning and dejected in losing.

This gives the contrast condition for A166:
truth-defense becomes spiritually dangerous when it turns into rank, identity, victory, and humiliation.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Four things must be separated

A166 distinguishes:

1. **the proposition** — true/false;
2. **the harm** — beneficial/harmful consequences;
3. **the corrective act** — what needs to be said/done;
4. **the mental stance toward the person** — goodwill, hatred, contempt, superiority.

A person may firmly reject (1), intervene because of (2), perform (3), while refusing hatred in (4).

This is CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS.

### B. Non-hatred is not neutrality between truth and falsehood

A166 rejects:
`mettā/non-hatred = treating every claim as equally acceptable`.

MN 58 and AN 5.198 preserve correction.
MN 103–104 preserve dispute diagnosis and resolution.

The early-text direction is:
**correct what should be corrected without feeding greed, hatred, conceit, or faction.**

### C. Harshness and firmness are not identical

Firmness can mean:
- clear boundary;
- explicit disagreement;
- refusal;
- consequence;
- disciplinary action;
- sustained correction.

Harshness adds:
- hostility;
- humiliation;
- contempt;
- aggression for its own sake.

This distinction is synthesis, but strongly constrained by MN 58, MN 21, and AN 5.198.

### D. Victory is a dangerous hidden objective

Aṭṭhakavagga adds a subtle diagnostic:
one may consciously say "I defend truth" while actually seeking:
- applause;
- superiority;
- defeat of opponent;
- group identity;
- protection of status.

A166 therefore asks whether the real objective is:
`truth/correction`
or
`victory/self-defense`.

### E. Reconciliation is not always required

Prior A160 remains binding:
non-hatred, forgiveness, trust, reconciliation, and restored access are distinct.

Applied here:
one can cease hatred and still:
- maintain distance;
- reject a harmful doctrine;
- impose boundaries;
- require evidence of change;
- decline reconciliation.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. AN 5.198 wording/numbering should be checked across editions before a lexical-level claim.
2. MN 103–104 primarily address monastic/community dispute contexts; general interpersonal extension is synthesis.
3. MN 21 sets an extreme training ideal and should not be misused to require passive exposure to abuse.
4. "Firmness vs harshness" is a functional synthesis, not a canonical binary term-pair.
5. Aṭṭhakavagga debate criticism does not imply a ban on all debate or public correction.
6. Full audit of `vivāda`, `kalaha`, `pharusa`, `mettā`, and admonition vocabulary remains OPEN.

## Practical understanding

Before correcting someone, ask:

1. What exactly is false or harmful?
2. What correction is actually needed?
3. Is now the right time?
4. Is the statement true?
5. Is it beneficial?
6. Can it be said without contempt?
7. Am I trying to correct the issue or defeat the person?
8. If the person refuses correction, what boundary or consequence is appropriate?
9. Can I maintain the boundary without cultivating hatred?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical nine-step list.
