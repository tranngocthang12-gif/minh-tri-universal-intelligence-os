## Research question

How can the Buddha teach right view and truth while simultaneously criticizing “only this is true” dispute and clinging to views? Is the target truth-claims themselves, or a particular relation to truth-claims?

## 1. Sn 4.8 Pasūra — debate is exposed as status competition

Sn 4.8 portrays disputants saying:
- only here is purity,
- other doctrines are not pure,
- their own dependent position is best,
- opponents are fools.

The discourse then focuses on the social-psychological fruits of debate:
- anxiety before judgment,
- humiliation after defeat,
- elation after praise,
- pride and conceit after victory.

The text explicitly says one should abstain from such disputes because their operative goal is praise/status rather than purification.

Important correction:
the target is not all reasoned disagreement. The target is debate structured around victory, praise, superiority and the possession of a view.

## 2. Sn 4.12 Cūḷaviyūha — “truth is one,” but sectarian ownership multiplies “truths”

Sn 4.12 presents rival teachers each living in their own view and saying:
- whoever knows this knows Dhamma,
- whoever rejects this is deficient,
- my doctrine is true and the opponent is foolish.

The Buddha’s reply includes the difficult statement:
**“truth is one, there is no second about which one who knows would dispute with one who knows.”**

The same poem explains that people make their own views into truths and use them to measure opponents.

Safe conclusion:
Sn 4.12 does **not** straightforwardly teach relativism (“everyone has their own equally true truth”).
It attacks the manufacture and possession of sectarian “own truths” that become bases for self-certification and contempt.

Translation/interpretive caution:
the exact semantic relation among sacca, pacceka-sacca/own truth, convention, perception and ultimate realization is disputed in scholarship. Do not collapse the poem into either simple absolutism or simple relativism.

## 3. Sn 4.13 Mahāviyūha — “only this is true” is criticized as a mode of attachment

Sn 4.13 asks about those who dwell in views and dispute saying “only this is true.”

The reply emphasizes:
- praise gained through dispute is small and not peace;
- one who knows does not become involved through preference for what is seen/heard;
- people call their own teaching perfect and others inferior;
- each treats its own agreed opinion/convention as truth;
- purity is not secured merely by saying “I know, I see, it is just so.”

Most important:
the poem repeatedly attacks **entrenchment** and **dependence**:
a person becomes hard to train when entrenched in their teaching and describes what they depend on as pure/true.

This makes the issue relational:
not “having cognition” but **being bound to cognition as identity/support**.

## 4. Sn 4.9 Māgandiya — the middle between “purity by views” and “purity by no views”

Sn 4.9 says purity is not found merely through:
- view,
- learning,
- knowledge,
- habits/practices,

but also not simply by lacking them.

This is decisive counterevidence against:
- “the Buddha rejected all views/knowledge”;
- “purity is attained by possessing the correct doctrine.”

The text instead associates peace with relinquishment/non-grasping and with the ending of equal/superior/inferior comparison.

## 5. MN 117 — right view is explicitly required

MN 117 makes right view the forerunner:
- discern wrong view as wrong;
- discern right view as right;
- right effort abandons wrong and develops right;
- right mindfulness keeps this task present.

Therefore any reading of Aṭṭhakavagga as “all view is bad” must explain why the early canon positively requires sammādiṭṭhi.

The strongest current reconciliation:
**right view is a path-function; view-clinging is a bondage-function.**

## 6. MN 22 — the raft and water-snake solve the apparent paradox

MN 22 gives two complementary lessons:

### Water-snake
Dhamma can be grasped wrongly:
- studied for attacking others,
- studied for defending oneself in debate,
- grasped without understanding its purpose.

Thus even correct teaching can become harmful when grasped for identity/status combat.

### Raft
Dhamma is taught for crossing over, not for carrying as possession after its function is complete.

Critical guardrail:
the raft simile does not mean “drop right view before using it.”
The discourse requires correct grasping for crossing and relinquishment after the function is fulfilled.

## 7. Truth vs possession of truth

Working distinction:

### Truth claim / right view
A claim or perspective can:
- discriminate wholesome/unwholesome,
- identify suffering and causes,
- guide conduct,
- direct the path toward cessation,
- be tested in practice.

### View-clinging / dogmatic ownership
A claim becomes bondage when:
- it defines “who I am,”
- status depends on winning,
- opponent disagreement is felt as self-threat,
- one needs praise/victory,
- one treats the formulation as sufficient for purity,
- one refuses correction because identity is invested.

This distinction is SYNTHESIS, but it explains the coexistence of Aṭṭhakavagga anti-clinging rhetoric and canonical right-view teaching without erasing either.

## 8. “Only this is true” is not identical with every statement “this is true”

The Aṭṭhakavagga critique occurs in a recurring social structure:
- “only our doctrine is pure,”
- others are inferior/foolish,
- debate for praise,
- conceit and comparison,
- dependence on a view.

Therefore it is too broad to infer:
“the Buddha never affirms truth/falsity.”

Sn 4.12 itself says “truth is one,” while other discourses define wrong/right view and the four noble truths.

The better target is:
**exclusive truth-possession as identity and competitive status.**

## 9. Scholarly tension

Modern scholarship is not unanimous.

One line of interpretation emphasizes that Aṭṭhakavagga radically destabilizes doctrinal truth-claims and language.

Another line, represented in Theravāda-oriented interpretation and some scholarship on right view, reads the poems as criticizing attachment to views rather than denying valid right view.

Paul Fuller’s study of diṭṭhi argues that right view is better understood as a detached mode/order of seeing rather than merely another proposition held in opposition to wrong views.

Current project stance:
keep the tension visible; do not resolve it by authority alone.
Use cross-text and cross-tradition evidence.

## 10. ARTHAPADA / T198 STATUS

T198 義足經 is an early Chinese translation of an Indic Arthapada recension corresponding broadly to the Aṭṭhakavagga and preserves sixteen discourses.

Exact verse-level alignment is already strong for some units such as Māgandiya and Kalahavivāda.

For Sn 4.12 and Sn 4.13 specifically, this pass did **not** independently verify a complete line-by-line T198 correspondence.

Therefore:
- **STRUCTURAL PARALLEL STATUS:** strong for Aṭṭhakavagga ↔ Arthapada as a corpus;
- **EXACT A2 VERSE ALIGNMENT:** OPEN pending dedicated T198 pass.

Do not claim exact Chinese lexical confirmation for “truth is one” or every view-dispute verse until verified.

## 11. MILINDA STRESS-TEST — debate itself is not the enemy

Milindapañha 3.1.3 distinguishes discussion “as scholars” from discussion “as kings.”

Scholar-discussion includes:
- refutation,
- counter-refutation,
- acknowledging error,
- drawing distinctions,
without anger.

King-discussion turns disagreement into punishment and power.

This is a strong later clarification:
reasoned disagreement is compatible with Buddhist inquiry when it is not identity-defense, anger or coercive status competition.

**MILINDA_COMPATIBLE.**

This is especially useful counterevidence against reading Sn 4.8–4.13 as a ban on rigorous dialogue.

## 12. FIRST A2 SYNTHESIS

A non-canonical model:

evidence / teaching
→ a view or working claim
→ skillful use: testing, discrimination, path-guidance
OR
→ appropriation: “my truth / my doctrine”
→ comparison: “we know, they are fools”
→ praise-seeking / fear of defeat
→ defensive debate
→ conceit, anger and further attachment.

The same representational content may function differently depending on relation/use.

This explains how:
- right view can be necessary,
- view-clinging can still be abandoned,
- truth can matter,
- debate-as-status-war can still be criticized.

## 13. COUNTEREVIDENCE / GUARDRAILS

Reject:
1. “The Buddha is a relativist: all views are equally true.” — Sn 4.12’s “truth is one,” right/wrong view distinctions and noble truths contradict this simplification.
2. “The Buddha is a dogmatist: correct doctrine should be defended as identity.” — Aṭṭhakavagga and MN 22 contradict this.
3. “All debate is bad.” — Milinda’s scholar-discussion and many canonical dialogues show constructive inquiry/refutation.
4. “Right view means merely memorizing correct propositions.” — MN 117 gives it practical path functions.
5. “The raft means reject teachings immediately.” — one must use the raft to cross before relinquishing it.
6. “Aṭṭhakavagga proves no truth can be spoken.” — the texts themselves make truth, causal and practical distinctions.
7. “T198 has already verified every A2 line.” — not yet; exact alignment remains pending.

## 14. CONFIDENCE

**EBT_STRONG**
- the canon positively teaches right view;
- view-clinging is something to abandon;
- Sn 4.8–4.13 target quarrel, praise, superiority, entrenchment and exclusive ownership of doctrine;
- MN 22 presents teachings as means for a liberating purpose rather than identity possessions.

**EBT_PROBABLE / SYNTHESIS**
- the central distinction is less “view vs no view” than **functional seeing vs identity-clinging**.
- “only this is true” is especially problematic when it functions as self/status ownership, not simply because truth-claims exist.

**MILINDA_COMPATIBLE**
- rigorous refutation without anger/status coercion is compatible with the broader anti-dispute ethic.

**OPEN**
- exact semantics of “truth is one” in Sn 4.12;
- exact historical relation between early Aṭṭhakavagga rhetoric and developed sammādiṭṭhi schemes;
- full T198 line-by-line confirmation for Sn 4.8/4.12/4.13.

## 15. PRACTICAL MEANING

Before defending a view, ask:
- Am I trying to discover what is true/useful, or to win?
- If evidence changes, can I revise the view without feeling personally diminished?
- Is disagreement threatening my claim, or my identity?
- Am I measuring myself as wiser/better than the other person?
- Has the doctrine become a tool for ending suffering, or a possession to defend?

The key practical distinction:
**one may use a map without making the map into oneself.**
