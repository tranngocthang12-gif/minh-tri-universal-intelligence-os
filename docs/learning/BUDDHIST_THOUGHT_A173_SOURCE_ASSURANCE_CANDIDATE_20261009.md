## Exact bounded research question

How does early Buddhist thought distinguish protective moral concern about doing wrong (`hiri/ottappa`) from `bhayabherava` (fear/dread) experienced in seclusion, and how should a practitioner respond without either fear-driven avoidance or reckless disregard of danger?

### E2 — MN 4, Bhayabheravasutta, early discourse (Pāli + English)

- Pāli, Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana-derived SuttaCentral archival edition: https://www.dhammatalks.net/suttacentral/sc2016/sc/pi/mn4.html
- English, Thanissaro Bhikkhu: https://www.dhammatalks.net/tipitaka/2Sutta-Pitaka/2Majjhima-Nikaya/Majjhima1/004-bhayabera-sutta-e2.html
- Checked Pāli phrase: `akusalaṃ bhayabheravaṃ` in passages attributing unwholesome fear/dread in forest seclusion to unpurified bodily conduct; verbal, mental and livelihood conduct follow.
- The discourse further treats greed, ill will, restlessness, conceit/self-praise, craving for honor, forgetfulness, weak concentration and insufficient wisdom as material to fear/dread in the relevant context.
- Later in this narrative the Bodhisatta dispels fear/dread in the posture in which it arose (walking, standing, sitting, lying down).
- **TEXT_ATTESTED in MN 4:** fear/dread here is bound to conduct, mental state and the specific context of solitude; fear can be met without reflexively switching posture.
- **GUARDRAIL:** this is not a blanket instruction to stay in physical danger or prohibit protective withdrawal. Generalization to all courageous action is CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS.

### E3 — MN 61, Ambalaṭṭhikarāhulovāda, early discourse (Pāli + English)

- Pāli, SuttaCentral archival edition: https://dhammatalks.net/suttacentral/sc2016/sc/pi/mn61.html
- English, Thanissaro Bhikkhu: https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN61.html
- Checked Pāli repetitive reflection formula: `paccavekkhitvā paccavekkhitvā` for bodily, verbal and mental action.
- **TEXT_ATTESTED:** evaluate anticipated, ongoing and completed actions for affliction of self, others or both; stop or avoid harmful action, acknowledge harmful bodily/verbal actions to teacher/wise companion, and restrain oneself in future. In the checked English rendering, harmful completed mental action should evoke distress/shame/disgust about it, followed by restraint.
- **CORRECTION GUARDRAIL:** do not misstate the teaching as 'all remorse or all shame should be immediately suppressed'; the passage makes moral recognition and future restraint consequential. Equally, do not identify the text's response with lifelong self-hatred or compulsive rumination; those are modern/cross-text interpretations.

## 2. New cross-text understanding (CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS)

A single English word 'fear' compresses distinct functions. What matters to a Buddhist ethical analysis is not merely intensity or the presence/absence of the feeling, but:

1. **Object:** wrongful action, a real situational danger, speculative misfortune, reputation, or self-positioning?
2. **Epistemic grounding:** what is directly known, what is inferred, and what is imagined?
3. **Conduct:** does it inhibit harmful action or obstruct a necessary ethical response?
4. **Causal conditioning:** is conduct/mental disposition contributing to fear, as examined in MN 4?
5. **Training response:** refrain from a harmful act; investigate mental fear without reflex avoidance; or prudently withdraw when genuine safety requires it.

The five-question model is an analytical synthesis, NOT a five-part taxonomy given by the Buddha.

**New guardrail for A173:** Moral caution (`ottappa`) and seclusion fear (`bhayabherava`) must not be collapsed into one phenomenon. The checked passages put them to different instructional uses. No strong claim is made that they cannot ever coexist.

**New guardrail for A172→A173 continuity:** Distinguish appropriate moral concern about wrongdoing from global self-condemnation; MN 61 positively instructs acknowledging harmful mental conduct and future restraint. Therefore 'healthy shame' cannot be replaced by an unqualified 'feeling shame is bad'.

## 3. Application stress test (CROSS_TEXT_APPLICATION)

A person must speak up to stop harmful conduct but fears public embarrassment. The model asks: Is the feared consequence harm to self/others, or mainly injury to status? Can they speak truthfully and without hatred? Is there an actually dangerous context requiring assistance or withdrawal? Choosing timing and means is compatible with courage. No single example proves the universal meaning of `ottappa`.
