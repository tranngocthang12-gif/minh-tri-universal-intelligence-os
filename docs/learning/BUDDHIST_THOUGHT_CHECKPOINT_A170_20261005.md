## Completed checkpoint A170

## Central question

After harming someone, what does genuine repair require from the wrongdoer, and what may remain outside the wrongdoer's control?

A170 separates:
- moral responsibility for what one did;
- attempts to repair what can be repaired;
- future restraint and changed conduct;
- the victim's independent decision about trust, access, reconciliation, or relationship.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. MN 61 — the wrongdoer must first see the act clearly

MN 61 requires reflection on whether an action led to harm.

When harm is recognized, bodily/verbal wrongdoing is not repaired by denial or self-justification. The training includes:
- acknowledging/disclosing the act;
- clarifying it with wise companions/teacher where appropriate;
- undertaking future restraint.

This gives A170 its first rule:

**repair begins with accurate recognition of the harmful act.**

### 2. DN 2 — growth begins when transgression is recognized as transgression

DN 2 preserves the recurring early Buddhist formula that recognizing a transgression as transgression, acknowledging it appropriately, and exercising future restraint supports growth in the discipline.

This blocks:
- euphemism;
- blame reversal;
- pretending the wrongdoing was harmless.

### 3. SN 42.8 — remorse cannot reverse the past

SN 42.8 emphasizes that past harmful deeds cannot be made not to have happened merely by remorse.

The appropriate direction is future restraint and abandonment of further harmful action.

A170 therefore distinguishes:
- **what can be repaired now**;
- **what cannot be undone**.

This is crucial because genuine repair does not require pretending the past was erased.

### 4. MN 65 — response to correction matters

MN 65 gives weight to whether a person:
- repeats misconduct;
- reacts angrily or evasively to correction;
- actually changes their ways.

Thus:
**repair claims are evaluated partly by how the person responds to accountability and correction.**

### 5. AN 4.192 — trust in character requires observation over time

AN 4.192 teaches that character-related qualities are known through sustained observation under relevant conditions.

Therefore:
**a repaired object or a spoken apology may be immediate; restored trust may require time.**

The trust application is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS, but the observation-over-time principle is text-attested.

### 6. AN 5.167 — correction/accusation must remain factual and benevolent

A person calling another to account should do so:
- at the right time;
- factually;
- gently;
- beneficially;
- with goodwill rather than hatred.

This remains relevant after harm:
repair cannot be forced through intimidation, humiliation, or manipulation.

### 7. A160 continuity — non-hatred does not compel reconciliation or restored access

A160 already established the project distinction among:
- non-hatred;
- forgiveness;
- reconciliation;
- trust;
- boundaries;
- changed-conduct evidence.

A170 carries this forward:
**the wrongdoer's duty to repair does not create a reciprocal duty in the harmed person to restore the relationship.**

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS, not a single early formula.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Four domains of repair

A170 distinguishes four domains:

1. **truth repair** — name the act accurately and stop distortion;
2. **material/practical repair** — restore, replace, compensate, or correct what can actually be corrected;
3. **behavioral repair** — future restraint and stable changed conduct;
4. **relational repair** — trust, access, reconciliation, or relationship restoration.

The first three primarily concern what the wrongdoer can undertake.
The fourth depends on the other person's freedom and safety as well.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### B. Apology is not a transaction that buys forgiveness

A170 rejects:
`I apologized -> you now owe me forgiveness/trust/access`.

An apology can be morally appropriate and still fail to restore what was damaged.

If the apology is used as leverage to demand restoration, it risks becoming another form of self-centered pressure.

### C. Some harms are only partially reparable

Objects may be replaced.
Money may sometimes be repaid.
False statements may be corrected.
Future conduct may change.

But:
- lost time;
- lost opportunities;
- trauma;
- death;
- broken safety;
- destroyed confidence;

may not be fully reversible.

A170 therefore distinguishes:
**repair effort** from **complete restoration**.

This is synthesis, but it follows SN 42.8's guardrail that the past cannot simply be undone.

### D. Genuine repair reduces burden on the harmed person

A170 proposes a diagnostic:

A wrongdoer is moving toward genuine repair when they increasingly carry the burden of:
- acknowledgment;
- correction;
- restitution where possible;
- future restraint;
- accepting reasonable consequences.

They are moving away from repair when they shift the burden back by demanding:
- reassurance;
- immediate forgiveness;
- secrecy;
- restoration of status;
- emotional care from the person harmed.

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION.

### E. Restitution without changed conduct is insufficient

Replacing what was lost while repeating the same harmful pattern is not full reform.

A170 links A169:
**repair of consequence and reform of conduct are distinct, and both may matter.**

### F. Changed conduct without repair may also be incomplete

A person can stop repeating the harmful behavior but still avoid:
- acknowledging specific harm;
- correcting lies;
- returning what can be returned;
- making practical amends.

Therefore behavioral improvement does not automatically erase outstanding responsibility.

This is synthesis, not a single canonical formula.

### G. The harmed person retains independent agency

A170's strongest synthesis:

**the wrongdoer controls repentance, restitution, restraint, and reform; they do not control whether another person restores trust or relationship.**

Demanding reconciliation can itself violate the boundary between responsibility and control.

## LATER/PARACANONICAL — MILINDAPAÑHA

Milindapañha material on:
- fault recognition;
- discipline;
- consequence;
- dismissal without anger;

supports the narrow point that correction and consequence need not be hostile.

It does not create a universal rule that the harmed person must reconcile after amends.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. The early discourses used here do not provide one general modern restitution code.
2. Exact material restitution duties vary by type of harm and may require Vinaya/legal-context audit.
3. "Trauma", "relational restoration", and "restitution" are modern analytic terms.
4. Some institutional harms require procedures beyond interpersonal apology.
5. Full Vinaya audit is needed before stronger claims about formal restitution after property/offense cases.
6. The harmed person's decision may itself be unwise or reactive; A170 only states that the wrongdoer does not own that decision.
7. A170 does not claim that reconciliation is bad; only that it is not mechanically owed by an apology.

## Practical understanding

For the person who caused harm:

1. What exactly did I do?
2. What part of the harm can still be corrected?
3. What material/practical restitution is possible?
4. What truth needs to be corrected publicly or privately?
5. What future restraint proves the pattern is changing?
6. What consequences do I need to accept?
7. Am I asking the harmed person to carry my guilt for me?
8. Am I treating apology as a purchase of forgiveness?
9. Can I continue repairing even if trust is not restored?
10. Can I accept that some consequences may remain permanently?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical ten-step list.
