## Central question

What do `hiri` and `ottappa` protect, and how do they differ from:
- feeling socially humiliated;
- protecting reputation;
- hating oneself;
- repetitive remorse (`kukkucca`)?

A172 treats English words such as "shame", "conscience", and "moral dread" as approximate glosses only. The Pāli terms remain primary.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. Hiri and ottappa are praised as protective moral qualities

Early-discourse material treats `hiri` and `ottappa` as wholesome qualities that protect conduct.

The project keeps the working distinction:
- `hiri`: an inward moral recoil from what is base, shameful, or unworthy;
- `ottappa`: caution/dread regarding wrongdoing and its consequences.

Guardrail:
these English glosses are functional approximations and do not establish exhaustive lexical definitions.

### 2. MN 61 — ethical review is keyed to harm, not appearance

MN 61 asks whether an intended, ongoing, or completed action leads to:
- self-affliction;
- affliction of others;
- affliction of both.

This gives A172 a decisive guardrail:

**moral restraint is evaluated by harmfulness and conduct, not merely by whether someone sees or judges you.**

### 3. A156 continuity — conduct when no one is watching matters

Prior project work already integrated hiri/ottappa with the question of conduct without surveillance.

The relevant early-text direction is:
ethical restraint should not depend only on:
- praise;
- blame;
- reputation;
- fear of exposure.

Thus:
`hiri/ottappa != image management`.

The explicit identity/reputation contrast is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### 4. Kukkucca/remorse is not identical to hiri/ottappa

Prior A131/A158 established that repetitive remorse/agitation can become a hindrance, while hiri and ottappa remain wholesome guardians.

Therefore the project must not collapse:

`hiri/ottappa = feeling bad for as long as possible`.

Moral sensitivity may prompt correction.
Persistent mental agitation is not itself proof of virtue.

### 5. MN 61 + SN 42.8 — after wrongdoing, the task shifts toward restraint

Once wrongdoing is recognized:
- acknowledgment matters;
- future restraint matters;
- self-consuming remorse does not undo the past.

This means hiri/ottappa are best understood functionally as qualities that help prevent and correct wrongdoing, not as mandates for permanent self-punishment.

### 6. SN 22.59 / SN 22.89 — moral fault should not be turned into a fixed self

SN 22.59 guards against appropriating aggregates as "mine / I / my self".
SN 22.89 distinguishes explicit self-view from subtler "I am" conceit.

Applied cautiously:
moral awareness can become distorted when:
- "this act was shameful"
turns into
- "my permanent essence is shameful."

The guilt-identity application is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Four phenomena must be separated

A172 distinguishes:

1. **moral sensitivity:** "this action is unworthy/harmful";
2. **consequence sensitivity:** "this wrongdoing leads to harmful results";
3. **status shame:** "others will think badly of me";
4. **identity shame:** "I am fundamentally contemptible."

The first two can function like hiri/ottappa.
The latter two are not established as equivalents.

### B. Hiri is not merely social embarrassment

If one behaves ethically only because:
- someone is watching;
- one's reputation is at stake;
- one fears humiliation;

then the regulating force is externally dependent.

A172's working synthesis is:
**hiri points more deeply toward moral unworthiness itself than toward public exposure alone.**

This is a guarded synthesis, not a final lexical definition.

### C. Ottappa is not simple fearfulness

Ottappa should not be collapsed into generalized anxiety or cowardice.

Its function is moral caution regarding wrongdoing and consequences.

Thus:
- fear of doing harm;
- fear of moral consequence;
are not identical to:
- fear of losing face;
- fear of disagreement;
- fear of social disapproval.

### D. Healthy moral shame points toward action

Healthy moral sensitivity tends toward:
- stopping wrongdoing;
- acknowledging it;
- repairing what can be repaired;
- future restraint.

Identity shame tends toward:
- hiding;
- paralysis;
- self-attack;
- status defense;
- reassurance seeking.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### E. Hiri/ottappa can coexist with dignity

A172 rejects:
`moral shame -> one must despise oneself`.

A person can say:
- "this action was beneath the standard I should live by";
while not saying:
- "therefore I am permanently beneath human worth."

The first supports correction.
The second freezes identity.

### F. Moral maturity reduces dependence on reputation

A useful synthesis from A129/A130/A156:
as moral discipline deepens, conduct should depend less on praise/blame and more on direct recognition of:
- harm;
- unwholesomeness;
- responsibility;
- consequences.

This is not indifference to community standards; it is a shift away from image-management as the primary motive.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. Full lexical audit of `hiri`, `ottappa`, `kukkucca`, and related shame/remorse vocabulary remains OPEN.
2. English "shame", "conscience", "moral dread", and "fear of wrongdoing" are imperfect glosses.
3. Modern psychological categories such as toxic shame or shame-identity are analytic overlays, not early Pāli terms.
4. Some social shame may occasionally support restraint, but it should not be equated wholesale with hiri.
5. Ottappa may involve concern about consequences in ways broader than a single modern fear-category.
6. SN 22.59/22.89 do not directly discuss modern shame psychology; identity application remains synthesis.

## Practical understanding

When feeling shame after wrongdoing, ask:

1. What exactly was unworthy or harmful in the action?
2. What consequence am I appropriately concerned about?
3. Would I still think the action wrong if nobody ever found out?
4. Am I trying to repair the harm, or mainly protect my reputation?
5. Has the moral signal already done its job by producing acknowledgment and restraint?
6. Am I now repeating self-attack after all useful correction has already been identified?
7. Can I reject the action strongly without turning that rejection into hatred of myself?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical seven-step list.
