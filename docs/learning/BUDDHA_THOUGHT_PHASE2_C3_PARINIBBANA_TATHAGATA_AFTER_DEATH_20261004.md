## Research question

Why are the four postmortem positions about the Tathāgata rejected? Does the extinguished-fire simile mean annihilation, untraceability, or something else? What can be said of an arahant while alive versus after final parinibbāna, and what—if anything—does parinibbāna add to nibbāna in the earliest material?

## 1. SN 22.85 Yamaka — annihilation is explicitly rejected

Yamaka holds the view:
an arahant, at the breakup of the body, is annihilated, perishes, and does not exist after death.

The text labels this a wrong/pernicious view.

Sāriputta dismantles it by asking whether the Tathāgata can be identified as:
- form,
- feeling,
- perception,
- formations,
- consciousness,
or as in, outside, the sum of, or wholly apart from the aggregates.

Yamaka answers no.

The conclusion is decisive:
if the Tathāgata cannot be pinned down in those ways even here and now, it is improper to assert that the arahant is annihilated and nonexistent after death.

**EBT_STRONG:** the early teaching is not simple annihilationism.

## 2. SN 22.86 Anurādha — even a “fifth answer” is rejected

Anurādha first tries to answer sectarians by saying the Tathāgata after death is described “otherwise than” the four positions.

The Buddha corrects him.

The same aggregate analysis follows:
the Tathāgata is not found as, in, apart from, the totality of, or outside the aggregates.

The Buddha then says:
**formerly and now, I describe only suffering and the cessation of suffering.**

This is crucial:
the teaching does not replace the four rejected metaphysical positions with a hidden fifth ontology.

The explanatory priority returns to:
- dukkha,
- its cessation.

## 3. SN 44.1 Khemā — the predicates fail because the basis of reckoning is gone

Khemā and later the Buddha give the same answer to King Pasenadi.

The form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness by which one would describe/reckon the Tathāgata have been abandoned at the root and are not subject to future arising.

Freed from those classifications, the Tathāgata is described as:
- deep,
- boundless/immeasurable,
- hard to fathom,
like the ocean.

Therefore:
- exists after death — does not apply,
- does not exist — does not apply,
- both — does not apply,
- neither — does not apply.

This is stronger than agnosticism.
The text gives a reason why the categories fail.

## 4. MN 72 — the fire simile is about fuel and inapplicability of directional predicates

Vacchagotta asks the same postmortem questions.

The Buddha first says those speculative positions are a thicket/fetter of views and do not lead to:
- disenchantment,
- dispassion,
- cessation,
- calm,
- direct knowledge,
- awakening,
- Nibbāna.

The Buddha then uses the fire simile.

A fire burns dependent on grass and timber.
When the fuel is exhausted and no more is supplied, the fire goes out.

If asked:
“Where did the extinguished fire go—east, west, north, south?”

Vacchagotta answers:
**that does not apply.**

The Buddha applies the same structure to the Tathāgata:
the aggregate bases by which the Tathāgata would be described are abandoned and not destined for future arising.

### Safe conclusion

The fire simile establishes:
1. dependence on fuel/conditions;
2. cessation when fuel is no longer supplied;
3. the directional “where did it go?” question becomes inapplicable.

### Unsafe conclusions

The simile by itself does not prove:
- “the arahant becomes absolutely nonexistent,”
- “the arahant survives as an invisible eternal entity.”

The text’s own point is non-applicability after the supporting basis is gone.

## 5. Fire and Nibbāna — extinction should be read with the fuel metaphor

Within the internal Buddhist logic:
- craving/clinging are fuel-like supports;
- renewed becoming depends on fuel;
- when fuel is exhausted, renewed becoming does not continue.

Thus “extinguishing” is not best interpreted as destroying a permanent self.
No permanent self has first been established.

It is:
**the ending of a conditioned burning/continuation because its nutriment has ended.**

This is a project synthesis grounded in MN 72 and dependent-arising material.

## 6. SN 22.87 Vakkali — Māra cannot find a location for consciousness

After Vakkali’s death, Māra searches:
“Where is Vakkali’s consciousness established?”

The Buddha says Vakkali attained final Nibbāna with consciousness unestablished.

This fits SN 22.53–54, where:
- passion for the aggregates provides support/landing for consciousness;
- when passion is abandoned, the support is cut off;
- consciousness is unestablished, not growing, and release follows.

### Guardrail

SN 22.87 should **not** be used by itself to prove:
“an eternal consciousness survives parinibbāna.”

The textual point securely established is:
**no establishment/support/location of consciousness is found in the way Māra seeks it.**

The ontological status beyond that remains open.

## 7. Ud 8.4 — no here, there, or between

Ud 8.4 says:
dependence → wavering;
non-dependence → no wavering;
then calm, no yearning, no coming/going, no passing away/arising, no here/there/between;
this is the end of suffering.

This converges with MN 72:
the final issue is not “which place did the liberated one go to?”

The framework of:
- location,
- motion,
- reappearance,
is itself withdrawn.

## 8. Iti 44 — living Nibbāna versus final no-residue ending

Iti 44 gives:
### With residue
An arahant is alive:
- faculties remain,
- pleasant/painful feeling can occur,
- greed/hate/delusion are extinguished.

### Without residue
At the end of the remaining life process:
- no future residue of conditioned becoming remains.

Therefore:
- realization of Nibbāna is possible before death;
- final parinibbāna marks the ending of the remaining conditioned life process and no renewed becoming.

### Terminology caution
The exact distinction between nibbāna and parinibbāna is not guaranteed to be technically identical in every early textual layer.
Do not impose later standardized vocabulary on every occurrence.

## 9. What parinibbāna “adds”

At the level of the current early-text model:

**Nibbāna while alive**
- defilements extinguished,
- clinging ended,
- future birth ended,
- present aggregates continue until life ends.

**Final parinibbāna**
- the present aggregate process reaches its biological end,
- no new aggregate process arises through renewed becoming.

This does not mean:
a self is first liberated and then destroyed.

It means:
the conditions for future renewed existence are already ended; at death, the remaining conditioned process is not replaced by a new birth.

## 10. Why this is not mere agnosticism

Mere agnosticism would say:
“We do not know whether the Tathāgata exists after death.”

The suttas go further:
- the four positions are repeatedly rejected;
- their practical drawback is explained;
- the aggregate basis of classification is analyzed;
- the fire/fuel analogy explains why destination language misfires.

Therefore the stance is better described as:
**category refusal grounded in the cessation of the conditions and bases by which a being is reckoned.**

## 11. Why this is not annihilationism

The strongest counterevidence is SN 22.85:
the statement that an arahant is annihilated, perishes, and does not exist after death is explicitly treated as a wrong view.

The problem is not merely that the wording is impolite.
Sāriputta dismantles the assumptions by aggregate analysis.

Thus:
**“parinibbāna = absolute nonexistence of a previously existing self” is not an early-Buddhist formulation.**

## 12. Why this is not eternalism

The opposite claim is also not affirmed.

The texts do not say:
- an eternal self remains,
- a personal consciousness inhabits an eternal realm,
- the Tathāgata continues as a definable being.

All ordinary bases of personal reckoning are said to be abandoned and not to arise again.

Thus:
**“parinibbāna = eternal personal survival” is also not an early-Buddhist formulation.**

## 13. Milindapañha stress-test — extinguished fire and unpointability

Milindapañha asks whether the Buddha can be pointed out as being “here or there” after final parinibbāna.

Nāgasena answers no and uses the extinguished-fire analogy:
once a flame has gone out, it cannot be pointed to in a direction.

This is strongly compatible with MN 72 and SN 44.

**MILINDA_COMPATIBLE:** no spatial pointing / no continued individual formation.

But Nāgasena’s philosophical formulation is later and should not be used to add a hidden ontology beyond the early texts.

## 14. Core distinction — untraceable is not the same claim as nonexistent

The early texts make the following safer distinction:

- **nonexistent** is one of the rejected postmortem predicates;
- **untraceable / not classifiable by the aggregate-based scheme** is closer to the textual logic.

Even “untraceable” is an explanatory gloss, not a canonical technical definition.

The project should therefore say:
**the texts deny the applicability of ordinary existence/nonexistence classification after final parinibbāna, rather than positively replacing it with either annihilation or eternal survival.**

## 15. COUNTEREVIDENCE / GUARDRAILS

Reject:
1. “The Buddha taught that an arahant becomes nonexistent after death.” — SN 22.85 explicitly rejects this.
2. “The Buddha taught that an arahant eternally exists after death.” — SN 44/MN 72 refuse this too.
3. “The fire simile proves annihilation.” — its explicit point is lack of fuel and inapplicability of directional questions.
4. “The fire simile proves hidden eternal survival.” — not stated.
5. “The Buddha merely did not know.” — texts provide a structural reason for not answering.
6. “There is a fifth hidden answer beyond the four.” — SN 22.86 corrects precisely this move.
7. “Unestablished consciousness in SN 22.87 proves an eternal consciousness.” — stronger than the text warrants.
8. “Parinibbāna is identical with ordinary death.” — Iti 44 distinguishes arahant liberation while alive from final no-residue ending.

## 16. CONFIDENCE

**EBT_STRONG**
- annihilationism is explicitly rejected.
- eternal postmortem personal existence is not affirmed.
- all four postmortem predicates are rejected.
- the rejection is grounded in aggregate-based reckoning and cessation of future arising.
- fire imagery emphasizes dependent burning and cessation with fuel exhaustion.
- arahant liberation is realized while alive; final parinibbāna involves no renewed becoming.

**TEXTUALLY STRONG IN PĀLI**
- SN 22.87’s unestablished-consciousness description.
- Iti 44’s two-element distinction.

**CROSS-TRADITION AUDIT STILL NEEDED**
- exact parallels for MN 72 / SN 22.85–87 / SN 44 cluster were not completed in this pass.

**MILINDA_COMPATIBLE**
- extinguished-fire analogy and inability to point to the Buddha “here or there.”

**OPEN**
- exact ontology, if any, beyond the rejected categories.
- exact historical semantic distinction nibbāna/parinibbāna across all early strata.
- how to interpret appatiṭṭhita viññāṇa without importing later theories.

## 17. Core synthesis

> In the early texts, final parinibbāna is not described as the annihilation of a self, because no permanent self has been established; nor as the eternal survival of a definable individual. The aggregates and conditions by which a being is reckoned no longer provide a future basis. The fire simile says that when fuel is exhausted, asking where the extinguished fire “went” misapplies the categories. The Buddha’s concern remains the ending of suffering and the ending of the conditions that sustain renewed becoming.

This is a SYNTHESIS, not a single canonical quotation.
