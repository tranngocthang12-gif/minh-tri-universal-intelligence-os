## Core question

When does concern about harmful consequences aid restraint and wisdom, and when does fear become status defense, rigid avoidance or reckless heroism? The categories in this question are a project *analytical framework*, not a canonical fourfold Buddhist taxonomy.

## Source log — exact ID and role

- `AN 2.9`, Lokapāla/Cariya (Pāli keyword `hirī ca ottappañca`): direct EARLY DISCOURSE. The two bright qualities protect ethical relationships. Public Pāli/translation comparison: https://www.buddha-vacana.org/sutta/anguttara/02/an02-009.html ; https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN2_9.html . English renderings vary (conscience/concern vs shame/compunction). This passage does **not** alone establish a complete modern definition of anxiety or cowardice.
- `MN 61`, Ambalaṭṭhikarāhulovāda, mirror analogy and examination of body/speech/mind before, during and after acting: direct EARLY DISCOURSE. It assesses potential and actual affliction of self, others or both, with restraint and acknowledgment of misconduct. Translation checked: https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/accesstoinsight/html/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html . This does not directly define `ottappa` or a probability-calculation method.
- `MN 4`, Bhayabherava, the bodhisatta's experience of fear in remote forest practice and resolving it without fleeing the posture in which it arose: direct EARLY DISCOURSE. Translation checked: https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.004.than.html . The passage concerns ascetic training and purification; it is **not** a rule requiring unprepared exposure to physical danger. The linked translation itself flags a possible updated edition.
- `Mil 5.2.3`, Death, Questions of King Milinda: LATER/PARACANONICAL, **consulted directly**. Nāgasena analyzes the apparent scope contradiction between 'all fear death' and arahants being beyond fear; he uses contextual counterexamples and distinguishes the general expression from its exceptions. https://dhammatalks.net/suttacentral/sc2016/sc/en/mil5.2.3.html . Later reasoning method, not a direct early-discourse quotation or warrant for the complete `ottappa` semantics.

## TEXT_ATTESTED (bounded)

T1: AN 2.9 explicitly pairs `hirī` and `ottappa` as guardians. No claim about English fear-subtypes follows directly from this short passage.
T2: MN 61 explicitly instructs examining foreseeable/actual harm when deciding, continuing or reviewing conduct.
T3: MN 4 narrates working through fear in a particular wilderness-practice context; it does not assert a universal 'never retreat' command.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS — not direct Buddha speech

S1: Ethical concern about consequences may support restraint; do not collapse it into cowardice.
S2: Facing fear within disciplined practice is distinguishable from impulsively facing a hazard to impress others.
S3: A reasoned withdrawal from danger can be compatible with conscientious conduct; this is a modern scenario interpretation, **not** a stated rule of MN 4.
S4: Treating an imagined possibility as certain is an *analytic inference* carried over from previous learning A146; it is not attested as the Buddha's probability theory.
