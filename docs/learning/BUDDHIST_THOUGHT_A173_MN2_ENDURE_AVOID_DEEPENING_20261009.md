## New question and correction

Earlier A173 contrasted fear of wrongdoing, prudent risk avoidance, imagined danger and prestige anxiety. It lacked a strong early-discourse example where **avoiding concrete danger** is explicitly appropriate. MN 2 provides that missing corrective countertext. Do not equate all withdrawal with cowardice, nor universalize an isolated-forest practice into a no-retreat safety rule.

## TEXT_ATTESTED (bounded, source-ID level)

- **MN 2 Sabbāsava Sutta (Majjhima 2), methods 4 and 5**: the discourse explicitly distinguishes āsavas abandoned through endurance and those through avoidance. Under `adhivāsanā`, there is appropriate endurance of cold, heat, hunger, unpleasant speech and painful feeling. Under `parivajjanā`, a monk wisely avoids an aggressive elephant/horse/dog, a snake, thorns, precipices and unsuitable places or company. Pāli spot check: `āsavā adhivāsanā pahātabbā`; `āsavā parivajjanā pahātabbā`; `paṭisaṅkhā yoniso ... parivajjeti`. Sources: https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN2.html and https://www.buddha-vacana.org/sutta/majjhima/mn002.html (side-by-side Pāli/English). This is a discourse on removing **āsavas**, not an exhaustive risk-management manual.
- **MN 4 Bhayabherava Sutta**: in an ascetic wilderness-fear setting, the Bodhisatta describes subduing fear without switching walking/standing/sitting/lying postures. The account includes prior attention to purity of body, speech, mind and livelihood. This is not a universal prohibition on avoiding dangerous animals. Source: https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.004.than.html.
- **MN 61 Ambalaṭṭhikarāhulovāda Sutta**: repeated review of possible and actual affliction before/during/after bodily, verbal and mental acts, with restraint and correction. It does not directly define ottappa nor assert numerical probabilities. Source: https://www.dhammatalks.net/accesstoinsight/html/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html.
- **AN 2.9 Lokapāla Sutta**: hiri and ottappa are two ethical guardians. This short passage does not establish an equivalence between every fear-like emotion and ottappa. Source: https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN2_9.html.

## MANDATORY MILINDAPAÑHA — LATER/PARACANONICAL, CONSULTED

- **Mil 5.2.3 — Death**: Nāgasena replies to a seeming conflict between 'all fear death' and arahant fearlessness by examining the relevant scope of 'all', using the tax-exempt ministers as a counterexample. He also gives a patient who desires cure but fears the surgeon's painful procedure. These are teacher-like *argument-analysis techniques*: scope, counterexample, and distinguishing a desired outcome from dread of a necessary process.
- Source: https://dhammatalks.net/suttacentral/sc2016/sc/en/mil5.2.3.html (Rhys Davids translation). This later example must not be silently labelled the direct early Buddha's statement, nor taken to establish every physiological aspect of fear.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS (not directly stated in these words)

S1. MN 2 + MN 4 show **different legitimate responses in different contexts**, so slogans 'always confront fear' and 'always avoid discomfort' are both overbroad. The shared decision-rule formulation is our inference; the two texts have different contexts and aims.
S2. The feared *object* and likely consequences matter more than the mere presence of the subjective feeling; consult MN 61's harm-review and MN 2's `yoniso` reflection. This is an interpretive bridge, not a canonical four-type taxonomy.
S3. A person may feel fear while taking responsible action; Mil 5.2.3 patient analogy helps explain this, but its provenance is later/paracanonical.
S4. Avoiding a genuine hazard is directly illustrated in MN 2; whether any **specific modern decision** counts as prudent avoidance requires independent factual assessment, not automatic appeal to a sutta.

## Countercheck — rival hypotheses

R1. 'If fear arises one must never retreat.' Refuted as a universal early-source claim by MN 2 method 5.
R2. 'Any unpleasant sensation should immediately be escaped.' Not supported: MN 2 method 4 discusses suitable endurance.
R3. 'MN 4 means face a live snake rather than withdraw.' Invalid context-transfer from forest fear training; MN 2 provides a direct animal-danger case.
R4. 'Every courageous person feels no fear.' Not established; Mil's later example shows desire for benefit can coexist with dread.
R5. 'Mil 5.2.3 teaches the original Buddha's precise lexical definition of ottappa.' Not established; the passage concerns context-specific fear of death.

## Worked applications — SYNTHESIS, not textual quotations

1. Someone encounters a dangerously aggressive dog: move to safety without needing to prove bravery. MN 2 supports the general *avoidance of real hazards*, not precise modern dog-handling instructions.
2. A monk in otherwise safe practice hears ordinary rustling and feels apprehension: observing the fear without compulsively changing posture resonates with MN 4, subject to real danger not being present.
3. Someone fears admitting a lie: MN 61's reflection on the act's effects makes responsible acknowledgment/restraint relevant; preserve the difference between harm-based moral caution and mere reputation management.
