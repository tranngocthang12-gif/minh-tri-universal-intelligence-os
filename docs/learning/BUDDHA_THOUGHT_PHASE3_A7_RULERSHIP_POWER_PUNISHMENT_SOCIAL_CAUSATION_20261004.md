## Research question

What makes political power righteous or unrighteous in early Buddhist thought? Why do DN 5 and DN 26 connect deprivation with crime? What are the limits of punishment? Does the Buddha teach a political program, or ethical constraints on rulership?

This pass stays within early Buddhist ethical logic and does not project modern political ideology onto the texts.

## 1. DN 26 — Dhamma is above the ruler

The ideal wheel-turning monarch is told to:
- depend on Dhamma,
- honor and revere Dhamma,
- take Dhamma as banner, authority and master.

This is the first decisive limit on kingship:
**the ruler is not the final moral authority.**

Power is legitimate only insofar as it is subordinated to Dhamma.

## 2. DN 26 — protection is broad, not only for elites

The ruler is instructed to provide righteous protection/security for:
- household/court,
- troops,
- nobles/vassals,
- brahmins and householders,
- town and country people,
- ascetics,
- even beasts and birds.

This broadens the object of royal duty beyond the ruler's own class.

The text also says:
- do not let adhamma/injustice prevail,
- provide wealth to those without wealth.

Thus royal duty includes both:
**protection from wrong** and **prevention of destitution.**

## 3. DN 26 — the ruler should submit judgment to ethical counsel

The ideal ruler is told to approach disciplined ascetics and brahmins and ask:
- what is skillful/unskillful,
- blameworthy/blameless,
- to be followed/not followed,
- what leads to long-term harm/sorrow,
- what leads to welfare/happiness.

Then the ruler should avoid what is unskillful and undertake what is skillful.

This means:
**power should remain teachable.**

The ruler is not depicted as morally self-sufficient.

## 4. DN 26 — failure to provide to the poor begins a social degeneration sequence

The king provides protection but fails to provide property to the penniless.

Then:
poverty spreads
→ theft spreads.

Initially the king gives money to thieves who say they cannot survive.
Others imitate theft to receive money.

The king then swings to the opposite extreme and executes a thief.
People respond by arming themselves and killing victims during robbery.

The discourse then traces further degeneration:
poverty
→ theft
→ weapons
→ killing
→ lying
→ further unskillful behavior.

The important structural point:
**social disorder is causally conditioned.**

The text does not explain violence only by individual evil character.

## 5. DN 26 also shows badly designed responses can worsen the problem

Two royal reactions both fail:
- indiscriminate reward after theft creates an incentive to steal;
- exemplary execution escalates the violence of robbery.

Therefore the discourse is more subtle than:
“give money to criminals”
or
“punish criminals harder.”

The prior duty is:
**prevent destitution before crime becomes the route to survival or reward.**

This is a project synthesis from the narrative sequence.

## 6. DN 5 — punishment alone does not uproot banditry

In DN 5's royal narrative, the country is troubled by robbery and disorder.

The royal priest explicitly says that trying to end the problem through:
- execution,
- imprisonment,
- confiscation,
- threats,
- banishment
will not fully end it, because survivors will continue to harass the realm.

The proposed alternative is:
- seed/fodder for farmers and cattle-keepers,
- capital for traders,
- food/wages for royal workers.

When people can engage in their occupations:
- robbery decreases,
- the realm becomes secure,
- the king's revenues increase.

The central insight:
**remove important conditions of disorder rather than relying only on punitive suppression.**

## 7. DN 5 is not a total abolition of punishment

The discourse criticizes punishment as insufficient to eradicate the root social problem.

It does not present a detailed doctrine:
“all punishment is always forbidden.”

Other early texts know and describe royal punishment.

Therefore the safe conclusion is:
**punishment is not an adequate substitute for addressing causes.**

Do not inflate this into a complete abolitionist legal theory.

## 8. SN 4.20 — an ideal of rule without killing, dispossession or causing sorrow

The Buddha reflects:
could rulership be exercised righteously:
- without killing or causing killing,
- without dispossessing or causing dispossession,
- without sorrowing or causing sorrow?

Māra then tempts him to rule.

The Buddha does not take up kingship.
He responds that even mountains of gold would not satisfy one person and that acquisition is a bond.

This produces a crucial two-level distinction:

### Political ethical ideal
Rulership is imagined under non-killing, non-dispossession and non-harm constraints.

### Liberative horizon
Even righteous acquisition/power is not the Buddha's final aim; renunciation of the bond of acquisition is higher.

Thus:
**good government is not identical with liberation.**

## 9. Power is morally constrained, not spiritually ultimate

Across DN 26 and SN 4.20:
- the ruler must submit to Dhamma,
- protect others,
- provide for the destitute,
- seek ethical counsel,
- avoid unskillful conduct.

But even an ideal wheel-turning monarch remains within conditioned worldly existence.

The highest path still points beyond:
- possession,
- domination,
- sensual acquisition,
- status.

This prevents turning Buddhist kingship into the final spiritual ideal.

## 10. The ruler's responsibility is causal, not only punitive

The ruler is not judged only by:
“did the king punish wrongdoers?”

The texts ask:
- were conditions of survival provided?
- was injustice allowed?
- were livelihoods supported?
- did royal conduct increase fear and violence?
- did the ruler listen to Dhamma?

This parallels the Buddha's wider method:
**look for conditions that sustain the problem.**

## 11. Individual responsibility is not erased

DN 26 still describes:
- theft as theft,
- killing as killing,
- lying as lying,
- unskillful action as unskillful.

So social causation does not mean:
“individual conduct no longer matters.”

The model is multi-level:
- individuals form intentions and act,
- rulers/social arrangements also condition which actions become likely and widespread.

This is an internal extension of dependent conditionality.

## 12. Non-harm and political force — an unresolved tension

SN 4.20 imagines righteous rule without killing/dispossession/harm.

DN 26 also demands righteous protection and prevention of injustice.

The early texts examined here do not give a complete constitutional/legal theory explaining every case where protection might seem to require coercion.

Therefore:
**the ideal of non-harm is strong, but the exact permitted scope of coercive force remains underdetermined.**

Do not claim:
- the Buddha endorsed all state violence,
or
- the Buddha gave a complete doctrine of absolute political pacifism.

## 13. Cross-tradition status

DN 26 has parallels/witnesses including:
- DĀ 6,
- MĀ 70,
- Sanskrit fragments.

DN 5 has:
- DĀ 23,
- Sanskrit witnesses/fragments.

This strengthens confidence that the broad social-causal and kingship material is not merely a late Theravāda-only creation.

A dedicated line-by-line parallel audit is still needed before claiming that every economic/punishment detail is identical across recensions.

## 14. What the Buddha does NOT give here

These texts do not provide:
- a modern constitution,
- electoral theory,
- separation of powers,
- capitalism/socialism,
- a complete criminal code,
- a modern human-rights doctrine.

What they do provide is an **ethical grammar of power**:
- Dhamma over ruler,
- protection,
- material provision,
- non-harm,
- causal understanding of disorder,
- willingness to receive ethical correction.

## 15. Core synthesis

> Early Buddhist thought treats political power as morally subordinate to Dhamma. A ruler is responsible not only for suppressing wrongdoing but also for the conditions that allow people to live without being driven toward crime. DN 5 and DN 26 show that deprivation, badly designed incentives and violent punishment can themselves become causes of further disorder. Yet the texts do not abolish individual responsibility or give a complete modern political program. The ideal is ethically constrained rulership; liberation still lies beyond acquisition and power.

This is a SYNTHESIS.

## 16. COUNTEREVIDENCE / GUARDRAILS

Reject:
1. "The Buddha taught punishment alone solves crime."
2. "The Buddha taught that criminals have no personal responsibility."
3. "The Buddha taught simply to give money after every crime." — DN 26 shows this can incentivize theft.
4. "The Buddha endorsed exemplary killing to deter crime." — the narrative shows escalation.
5. "The Buddha gave a full modern welfare-state program."
6. "The Buddha gave a complete abolitionist criminal-justice theory."
7. "A righteous king is the highest Buddhist ideal." — SN 4.20 keeps acquisition/power below renunciation.
8. "Dhamma is whatever the king declares." — DN 26 explicitly places Dhamma over the ruler.

## 17. CONFIDENCE

**EBT_STRONG / PĀLI TEXTUALLY STRONG**
- DN 26 places Dhamma above rulership.
- the ideal ruler owes broad protection and material provision to the destitute.
- social deprivation is presented as a condition for theft and wider disorder.
- DN 5 says punitive suppression alone does not uproot the problem and prescribes livelihood support.
- SN 4.20 imagines righteous rulership under non-killing/non-dispossession/non-sorrow constraints while treating acquisition as bondage.

**CROSS-TRADITION STRONG AT CORPUS LEVEL**
- DN 26 ↔ DĀ 6 / MĀ 70 / Sanskrit witnesses.
- DN 5 ↔ DĀ 23 / Sanskrit witnesses.
