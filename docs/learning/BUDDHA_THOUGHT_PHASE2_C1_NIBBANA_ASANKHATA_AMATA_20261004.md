## Research question

What does it mean, within early Buddhist thought itself, to call Nibbāna:
- asaṅkhata / unconditioned,
- amata / deathless,
- taṇhakkhaya / destruction of craving,
- virāga / dispassion,
- vimutti / release?

How can a conditioned path lead to what is called unconditioned? Is Nibbāna best understood as a “thing,” a place, a cessation, a realization, or a cluster of early expressions that should not be collapsed too quickly?

## 1. SN 43 — the safest starting point is functional, not substantial

SN 43 repeatedly asks what the unconditioned is and gives the answer:
**the ending of greed, hate and delusion.**

It then gives paths leading to the unconditioned.

This creates a crucial internal distinction:
- the path is cultivated and conditioned;
- the goal is called unconditioned;
- the early text does not say the path “manufactures” the unconditioned as one more fabricated phenomenon.

The safest reading is therefore:
**practice conditions realization/attainment of the unconditioned; it does not turn Nibbāna into a produced saṅkhata object.**

## 2. SN 43 also groups many goal-terms around one liberating reality/function

Across the Asaṅkhata-saṃyutta, closely related names include:
- asaṅkhata,
- amata,
- taṇhakkhaya,
- virāga,
- vimutti,
- nibbāna,
and other terms of peace, security, refuge and final good.

Important:
this does not prove that every term is an exact dictionary synonym in every occurrence.

But it does show that early Buddhist language can approach the same goal from different sides:
- by what ends,
- by what is escaped,
- by the quality of peace,
- by the contrast with conditioned existence.

## 3. Amata — “deathless” should first be read through the ending of birth

MN 26 presents the noble search:
being oneself subject to birth, aging, illness, death, sorrow and defilement, one seeks:
- the unborn,
- unaging,
- unailing,
- deathless,
- sorrowless,
- undefiled,
- unsurpassed security from bondage: Nibbāna.

Within the same early framework, liberation ends renewed birth.

Therefore the strongest internal reading of amata is:
**what is free from the cycle that produces renewed birth and death.**

Unsafe leap:
“deathless” does not by itself establish an immortal personal consciousness or eternal self.

Snp 5.9 also identifies the dispelling of desire and passion for what is seen, heard, sensed and cognized as the “deathless Nibbāna,” reinforcing the link between amata and ending attachment.

## 4. Udāna 8.3 — positive language about the unconditioned, but not a self-doctrine

Ud 8.3 states that if there were no:
- unborn,
- unbecome,
- unmade,
- unconditioned,
escape from the born, become, made and conditioned would not be discernible.

This is one of the strongest positive contrasts in the early Nibbāna material.

What it establishes:
- escape is possible because the goal is not merely another member of the conditioned class.

What it does **not** establish:
- a permanent ātman,
- a cosmic self,
- a spatial heaven-substance,
- an eternal individual consciousness.

The text marks a contrast of conditioned/unconditioned; it does not supply a substantialist metaphysics.

## 5. MN 140 — Nibbāna is also described through peace and ending conceiving

MN 140 identifies the ultimate noble peace with the pacification of:
- greed,
- hate,
- delusion.

It also treats:
- “I am,”
- “I am this,”
- “I will be,”
- “I will not be,”
- future identity in terms of form/perception,
as forms of maññanā/conceiving.

Going beyond all such conceiving characterizes the sage at peace.

This is highly significant:
Nibbāna is not presented as acquisition of a perfected metaphysical identity.

Rather, peace and release appear together with the ending of:
- greed,
- hatred,
- delusion,
- self-referential conceiving,
- renewed birth.

## 6. Iti 44 — two Nibbāna elements distinguish liberation while alive from final no-residue cessation

Iti 44 distinguishes:
### sa-upādisesa nibbānadhātu
The arahant is alive:
- faculties remain,
- pleasant and painful feeling may still occur,
- greed/hate/delusion are extinguished.

### anupādisesa nibbānadhātu
At the end of bodily life:
- no residue remains for future continued modes of being.

This makes an important point:
**Nibbāna is not first attained only at physical death.**

The destruction of defilements is realizable while alive.

Final parinibbāna concerns the end of the remaining conditioned life process.

Text-critical caution:
the exact antiquity and cross-tradition status of this two-element taxonomy still requires separate comparative audit.

## 7. SN 44 — why “exists after death / does not exist” fails

SN 44.1 refuses all four:
- Tathāgata exists after death,
- does not exist,
- both,
- neither.

The explanation is tied to the five aggregates:
those bases by which the Tathāgata could be reckoned/described are abandoned, destroyed at the root, and not destined for future arising.

Thus the refusal is not merely:
“the Buddha has no opinion.”

The structure is:
ordinary postmortem predicates depend on a way of reckoning a being through the aggregates; when that basis is no longer applicable, the fourfold question does not properly apply.

Therefore:
- eternalist survival is not affirmed;
- annihilationism is not affirmed;
- the question-frame itself is being dismantled.

## 8. Nibbāna is not “mere nothingness”

Several early strands prevent this reduction:
- SN 43 positively names the unconditioned and gives a path to realization.
- MN 26 makes Nibbāna the noble object of search.
- Ud 8.3 says escape would be impossible if there were no unborn/unconditioned.
- Iti 44 distinguishes realization while alive from final no-residue Nibbāna.

So “Nibbāna = nothing at all” is too crude.

But this does **not** authorize replacing “nothingness” with:
“an eternal substance.”

The early material is more disciplined than either extreme.

## 9. Nibbāna is not simply a “place”

The geographical places:
- Bodh Gaya/Uruvelā,
- Kusinārā/Kushinagar,
are places where awakening and final parinibbāna events occurred.

They are not presented as spatial locations in which Nibbāna itself is stored.

This distinction remains consistent with later Milindapañha clarification.

## 10. Milindapañha stress-test

### MILINDA_COMPATIBLE — path to realization is not production of Nibbāna
Nāgasena distinguishes:
- a path/cause for realizing Nibbāna,
from
- a cause that produces Nibbāna.

This is later and more systematic, but it fits SN 43 well:
the path leads to the unconditioned.

### MILINDA_COMPATIBLE — no fixed spatial location
Milinda asks where Nibbāna is located.
Nāgasena denies a fixed directional/place location while maintaining that Nibbāna can be realized.

This is a useful clarification of a category mistake:
**realizability does not require spatial storage.**

### MILINDA_EXTENSION — philosophical proof of Nibbāna’s reality
Milinda develops analogies and arguments more explicitly than the early suttas.

Use:
- to clarify the logic,
not
- to define the historical Buddha’s exact ontology.

## 11. A careful internal taxonomy of Nibbāna language

### A. Cessation language
- greed ends,
- hatred ends,
- delusion ends,
- craving ends,
- clinging and renewed becoming cease.

This is the most secure level.

### B. Liberation-process language
- knowing-and-seeing,
- nibbidā,
- virāga,
- vimutti.

This describes how the practitioner arrives at release.

### C. Unconditioned contrast language
- unborn,
- unbecome,
- unmade,
- unconditioned,
- deathless.

This describes the goal by contrast with conditioned arising and dying.

### D. Postmortem limit language
- ordinary “exists/does not exist” predicates do not apply straightforwardly to the liberated Tathāgata.

These four registers should not be collapsed into one simplistic definition.

## 12. How can a conditioned path lead to the unconditioned?

This is the central philosophical question of C1.

The early texts allow this answer without contradiction:

- right view, virtue, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom are conditioned;
- these conditioned practices remove the conditions of greed, hatred, delusion, craving and clinging;
- when the conditions sustaining saṃsāric becoming are ended, the unconditioned is realized.

Thus:
**the conditioned path removes conditions; it need not fabricate the unconditioned.**

This is an internal Buddhist synthesis strongly supported by SN 43 and the cessation structure.

## 13. Relation to the previous rebirth block

Phase 2B established:
continuity persists through conditions without a permanent carrier.

Phase 2C now adds:
when the conditions sustaining continuity cease, renewed birth ceases.

This makes Nibbāna intelligible within the same dependent-arising thought:
- saṃsāra = conditioned continuation;
- Nibbāna = ending of the conditions that bind continuation to craving/ignorance.

No permanent self must be destroyed.

## 14. COUNTEREVIDENCE / GUARDRAILS

Reject:
1. “Nibbāna is just physical death.” — arahants realize Nibbāna while alive.
2. “Nibbāna is only psychological calm.” — too narrow for deathless/unborn/unconditioned and end-of-rebirth language.
3. “Nibbāna is an eternal soul.” — not supported and conflicts with anattā/conceiving analysis.
4. “Nibbāna is sheer nonexistence.” — too crude for SN 43, MN 26 and Ud 8.3.
5. “The path produces Nibbāna as a conditioned object.” — stronger than the early wording permits.
6. “Because Nibbāna is unconditioned, no practice is needed.” — SN 43 explicitly teaches paths leading to realization.
7. “After parinibbāna the Buddha definitely exists in an eternal realm.” — SN 44 does not affirm this.
8. “After parinibbāna the Buddha simply becomes nonexistent.” — SN 44 does not affirm this either.

## 15. CONFIDENCE

**EBT_STRONG**
- Nibbāna is functionally identified with ending greed/hate/delusion.
- the goal is called unconditioned and deathless.
- a path is taught leading to realization of the unconditioned.
- noble search seeks unborn/deathless Nibbāna.
- postmortem existence/nonexistence predicates are not endorsed.
- Nibbāna can be realized while alive.

**PĀLI-STRONG / CROSS-TRADITION AUDIT PENDING**
- exact two-Nibbāna-element taxonomy of Iti 44.

**MILINDA_COMPATIBLE**
- path conditions realization but does not manufacture Nibbāna;
- Nibbāna lacks a fixed spatial location.

**OPEN**
- whether all positive unconditioned terms should be read literally, analogically, or functionally in every context;
- exact historical relation between nibbāna/parinibbāna usage across early strata;
- the fullest ontological claim that can legitimately be made from Udāna 8 without importing later doctrine.

## 16. Core synthesis

The strongest current formulation is:

> Nibbāna in early Buddhist thought is first and most securely understood as the cessation of greed, hate, delusion, craving and renewed becoming. It is called unconditioned and deathless because it is not another conditioned state within the birth-and-death process. The path is conditioned, but it leads by removing the conditions of bondage; it does not need to manufacture Nibbāna as a new fabricated thing.

This is a SYNTHESIS, not a single canonical quotation.
