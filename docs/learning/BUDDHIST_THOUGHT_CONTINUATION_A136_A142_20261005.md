## A136 — subtle conceit in humility and non-attachment
Anchors: MN 113, SN 22.89, MN 22, Atthakavagga.
Core: even humility, simplicity, renunciation, or “not caring about status” can become identity if appropriated as “I am the one who is humble/non-attached.”
Milindapanha: later anti-pride and conventional-designation support only.

## A137 — upekkha vs indifference
Anchors: brahmavihara formulas, SN 46, jhana/feeling material.
Core: equanimity is balanced non-reactivity that can coexist with compassion, effort, boundaries, and responsibility; it is not coldness or resignation.
Milindapanha: later support only.

## A138 — karuna vs rescuing/control
Anchors: brahmavihara compassion, SN 47.19 Sedaka, Dhammapada 166, right effort.
Core: compassion seeks reduction of suffering but does not imply control, unlimited self-sacrifice, or ownership of another's outcome. Self-protection and other-protection can be reciprocal.
Milindapanha: later non-harming/restraint support only.

## A139 — metta vs liking/approval/access
Anchors: mettā formulas, MN 21, AN 5.161, AN 5.167, MN 58.
Core: goodwill can coexist with dislike of conduct, boundaries, consequences, correction, and opposition. Mettā does not require approval, intimacy, trust, or conflict-avoidance.
Milindapanha: later corrective-speech and non-harming support only.

## A140 — mudita vs excitement/flattery/comparison
Anchors: brahmavihara mudita, early issa/macchariya material, mana/comparison and praise/status material.
Core: mudita rejoices in another's genuine welfare without converting it into self-ranking, reflected status, or competitive threat. The anti-envy relation is treated as cross-text/later-systematic support, not a single early-text definition.
Milindapanha: limited later anti-pride support.

## A141 — four brahmaviharas as a relational system
Anchors: standard formulas, MN 62, right effort, right speech/action.
Working synthesis:
- mettā: baseline non-hostility/welfare;
- karuṇā: foreground suffering/harm;
- muditā: foreground genuine welfare/wholesome success;
- upekkhā: balance amid uncertainty, preference, comparison, and limits of control.
This is a CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS, not a canonical decision tree.
Milindapanha: limited later support only.

## A142 — brahmaviharas and the three unwholesome roots

Central question:
How do mettā, karuṇā, muditā, and upekkhā interact with lobha, dosa, and moha, and why is goodwill alone insufficient without wisdom?

### TEXT_ATTESTED

1. Early Buddhism repeatedly treats greed/lust (lobha/rāga), hatred/ill will (dosa/dvesa/byāpāda), and delusion (moha/avijjā) as fundamental unwholesome roots or drivers of unskillful action.
2. MN 62 functionally pairs mettā with abandonment of ill will, karuṇā with abandonment of cruelty, muditā with abandonment of discontent, and upekkhā with abandonment of aversion. These are direct anti-hostility functions but do not by themselves amount to a full cure for delusion.
3. Right view and wise attention remain indispensable. A mind can be benevolent in intention yet still misjudge causes, consequences, timing, or what is actually beneficial.
4. MN 58 and MN 61 remain important guardrails: compassionate intent does not bypass truth, benefit, timing, harm review, or correction.
5. AN 3.65 and other early discernment material emphasize knowing wholesome/unwholesome by roots and consequences, preventing a purely sentimental ethics.

### GOODWILL IS NOT ENOUGH IF UNDERSTANDING IS WRONG

A142's core distinction:

- wholesome affective orientation: non-hatred, compassion, rejoicing, balance;
- wisdom/discernment: correctly seeing causes, consequences, and what actually reduces suffering.

One can sincerely mean well and still:
- enable harm;
- misread a situation;
- give the wrong help;
- protect an unhealthy pattern;
- confuse attachment with care.

These are CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION examples.

Therefore:
**good intention != guaranteed good outcome**
and
**goodwill != right view**.

### HOW THE FOUR RELATE TO THE ROOTS

A guarded working map:

- mettā primarily counters ill will/hatred;
- karuṇā primarily counters cruelty and callousness;
- muditā weakens envy, resentment, and competitive hostility;
- upekkhā weakens reactive attraction/aversion and helps prevent preference from distorting judgment.

But lobha and moha can still infiltrate all four if wisdom is absent.

This map is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS with direct support strongest for the MN 62 pairings.

### LOBHA CAN DISGUISE ITSELF AS CARE

Examples:
- “I love you” but need possession;
- “I help you” but need gratitude;
- “I rejoice for you” but want reflected status;
- “I am equanimous” because withdrawal protects my comfort.

Thus pleasant or caring language does not prove freedom from greed.

A142 therefore tests:
**what does the mind want to obtain, preserve, or own?**

### DOSA CAN DISGUISE ITSELF AS MORAL CLARITY

Earlier checkpoints showed that one can correctly identify wrongdoing while still adding hostility.

Examples:
- correction mixed with contempt;
- justice mixed with delight in punishment;
- boundaries mixed with revenge.

So accurate moral judgment does not automatically purify the emotional root.

A142 preserves:
**seeing wrong clearly != needing hatred to oppose it.**

### MOHA IS THE DEEPEST GUARDRAIL

The brahmavihāras can soften hostility and self-centered reaction, but without wisdom one may still misunderstand:
- what suffering is;
- what causes it;
- what action is helpful;
- what is under one's control;
- what is self/mine;
- what consequences follow.

Therefore moha cannot be solved by kindness alone.

A142's key synthesis:
**the heart may be kind while the view is confused.**

This is why right view, wise attention, mindfulness, and investigation remain necessary.

### BRAHMAVIHĀRAS + WISDOM

A142's integrated model:

- mettā keeps discernment from becoming hostile;
- karuṇā keeps discernment oriented toward reducing suffering;
- muditā keeps discernment from being distorted by envy/comparison;
- upekkhā keeps discernment stable under gain/loss and uncertainty;
- wisdom keeps all four from becoming naive, possessive, sentimental, or misdirected.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS, not a canonical five-part formula.

### PRACTICAL DIAGNOSTIC

Before acting from “goodwill,” ask:
1. Is there any hidden gain I want?
2. Is resentment or contempt mixed into the response?
3. What facts do I actually know?
4. What am I assuming?
5. What action is truly beneficial rather than merely pleasant?
6. Could my help enable further harm?
7. Am I respecting the other person's agency?
8. What outcome is actually under my influence?
9. Does the response reduce greed, hatred, and delusion—or only one of them?
10. What would wise attention ask me to notice that emotion alone misses?

### MILINDAPANHA — MANDATORY

Consulted: YES.

Relevant later support:
- repeated distinction between reasoning and wisdom;
- wisdom as cutting/illuminating what other wholesome qualities alone do not remove;
- non-harming and loving-kindness material as support for the affective side.

Role: later/paracanonical clarification only.

Guardrail:
Milindapanha does not replace early right-view, wise-attention, MN 62, MN 58, or MN 61 material.

### CORRECTIONS PRESERVED

- kindness != wisdom;
- goodwill != right view;
- compassion != guaranteed benefit;
- non-hatred != accurate judgment by itself;
- lobha can hide inside attachment called “care”;
- dosa can hide inside “righteousness”;
- moha can remain even in a gentle mind;
- brahmavihāras and wisdom are complementary, not substitutes.
