## Question

How does feeling (vedanā) condition craving (taṇhā), and does the early-text formula vedanāpaccayā taṇhā mean that every feeling mechanically produces craving?

## TEXTUAL FINDING

### SN 36.6 / SĀ 470 — the two arrows
Both the Pāli Sallattha-sutta and its Saṃyukta-āgama parallel preserve the core distinction:
- ordinary and trained persons both experience pleasant, painful and neutral feeling;
- with painful bodily feeling, the untrained person adds sorrow/confusion and aversion;
- the trained noble disciple can experience the bodily pain without the second mental arrow;
- the untrained person also seeks sensual pleasure as an escape from pain and remains bound by passion, aversion and ignorance;
- the trained disciple knows feeling's arising, passing, gratification, drawback and escape and is not bound by the same tendencies.

This is strong evidence that liberation does not require eliminating vedanā itself.

### MN 148 / SĀ 304 — sixfold sense-process
MN 148 analyzes:
sense base + object → consciousness → contact → feeling → craving.

More importantly for practice, it specifies conditional reactivity:
- pleasant feeling, when relished/welcomed/fastened to, activates the tendency to passion;
- painful feeling, when followed by sorrow/resistance, activates the tendency to aversion;
- neutral feeling, when not understood in terms of arising/passing/gratification/drawback/escape, activates the tendency to ignorance;
- corresponding non-reactive/knowing modes do not activate those tendencies.

SĀ 304 is a recognized parallel to MN 148 and preserves the six-six framework (six internal bases, external bases, consciousnesses, contacts, feelings, cravings) and the not-self analysis, although the extant Chinese presentation is not identical in every expansion.

### SN 12.2 / SN 12.20 / SN 12.44
The standard dependent-arising formula preserves:
phassa → vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna → bhava → jāti → dukkha.

SN 12.20 frames the relation as dhamma regularity/this-that conditionality. SN 12.44 maps the same sequence directly onto each of the six sense doors.

### MN 44 — critical counterevidence to mechanical determinism
MN 44 explicitly asks whether the underlying tendency to passion/aversion/ignorance accompanies every pleasant/painful/neutral feeling and answers no. It gives examples of non-sensual pleasant feeling in meditative development.

Therefore the formula vedanāpaccayā taṇhā should not be treated as a claim that every occurrence of feeling inevitably and immediately generates craving.

## PARALLEL STATUS

- SN 36.6 ↔ SĀ 470: strong direct parallel; the two-arrow structure is preserved cross-tradition.
- MN 148 ↔ SĀ 304; also Tibetan Up 3059: recognized parallel family, with differences in extent/detail.
- MN 44 has Madhyama-āgama and Tibetan parallels; the exact relevant passage should be separately aligned before using it as cross-tradition proof for every detail.
- SN 12.2 has an EĀ parallel for the dependent-arising analysis.

## INTERPRETATION

The practical hinge is not “stop feeling.” It is whether feeling is followed by appropriation/reactivity.

A useful working model is:
- pleasant → relishing / holding → passion;
- painful → resistance / mental proliferation, often followed by compensatory sensual seeking → aversion + passion;
- neutral → non-understanding / inattentiveness → ignorance.

This is a synthesis of multiple passages, not one canonical formula.

The “second arrow” should not be reduced to “distress is simply a voluntary choice.” The texts contrast an untrained person with a trained noble disciple; the difference is cultivated understanding and non-reactivity, not mere willpower.

## MILINDA STATUS

### MILINDA_COMPATIBLE — strong
Mil 3.6.7 distinguishes the person with passion from one without passion while both taste food: the first experiences taste plus lust; the passion-free person experiences the taste without the lust that arises from taste. This strongly matches the early-text distinction between vedanā and craving/reactivity.

### MILINDA_COMPATIBLE — strong
Mil 3.2.4 says one who will not be reborn may still feel bodily pain but not mental agony, explicitly echoing the one-arrow/two-arrow distinction.

### MILINDA_COMPATIBLE
Milindapañha passages also restate the sense-base → consciousness → contact → feeling → craving → clinging → becoming sequence.

No Milinda passage used here is treated as independent proof of what the historical Buddha said; it functions as later clarification/stress-test.

## COUNTEREVIDENCE / GUARDRAILS

Reject these overstatements:
1. “Feeling itself is the problem.” — contradicted by trained/arahant feeling without corresponding bondage.
2. “Every pleasant feeling creates lust.” — MN 44 explicitly denies this.
3. “Every painful feeling necessarily creates aversion.” — SN 36.6/SĀ 470 deny this for the trained noble disciple.
4. “The second arrow is always a simple conscious choice.” — not supported; the texts emphasize training, understanding and underlying tendencies.
5. “Vedanā is identical with a full emotion.” — too broad; the texts distinguish feeling tone from subsequent sorrow, resistance, delight and other reactions.

## CONFIDENCE

**EBT_STRONG**
- feeling remains for the trained/liberated person;
- painful feeling need not generate the second mental arrow;
- pleasant/painful/neutral feelings have characteristic risks of passion/aversion/ignorance;
- the vedanā → taṇhā link is conditional, not a simplistic one-to-one mechanical reflex.

**EBT_PROBABLE / MODEL**
- describing this as a “functional decoupling point” in the present moment is useful, but is interpretive language rather than a canonical technical term.

## PRACTICAL MEANING

When a noticeable experience occurs, distinguish:
1. contact/object;
2. affective tone: pleasant / unpleasant / neutral;
3. the next reaction: grasping, resistance, stimulation-seeking, identity story.

The empirical question is not “Can I have no feeling?” but:
> Can the feeling be known without automatically turning into thirst, resistance or ignorance?

This is a practice hypothesis, not a substitute for textual evidence.
