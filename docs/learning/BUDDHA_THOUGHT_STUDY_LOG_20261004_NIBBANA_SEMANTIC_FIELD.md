## Research question

What claims about Nibbāna are strongly textual, what are metaphorical or functional descriptions, what are later systematizations, and what must remain open? How do nibbāna, asaṅkhata, amata, taṇhakkhaya, virāga, vimutti and anupādisesa relate?

## TEXTUAL FINDING

### 1. SN 43: many names, one functional core

SN 43 (Asaṅkhata-saṃyutta) applies many names to the goal, including:
- asaṅkhata / unfabricated-unconditioned,
- amata / deathless,
- taṇhakkhaya / ending of craving,
- virāga / dispassion,
- vimutti / release,
- nibbāna / unbinding,
- peace, refuge, island, shelter, security, ultimate.

In the collection, these names are repeatedly defined by the same functional core:
**ending of passion, ending of aversion, ending of delusion.**

This is decisive against treating each epithet as a separate metaphysical entity. They are multiple angles on the same liberating goal.

### 2. Asaṅkhata does not mean “a permanent self”

SN 43 calls the goal asaṅkhata and connects it to the ending of greed/hatred/delusion.
Udāna 8.3 adds the formula:
unborn / unbecome / unmade / unconditioned.

Safe conclusion:
Nibbāna is not described as a conditioned event produced in the same way as ordinary saṅkhata phenomena.

Unsafe inflation:
this does not by itself establish an eternal substance, cosmic Self or soul that persists as an entity.

### 3. Amata / deathless

SN 43 includes “deathless” among the names for the goal.

Working interpretation:
“deathless” functions as a liberation epithet associated with the ending of the processes that generate renewed birth and death.

Do not equate amata with “an immortal personal consciousness.” The surrounding no-self and postmortem tetralemma material blocks that move.

### 4. Taṇhakkhaya / ending of craving

SN 43 explicitly lists the ending of craving as a name for the goal.

This links the liberation module directly back to the vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna analysis:
Nibbāna is not merely a state reached after the causal system has done its work; its functional description is the ending of a core causal driver within that system.

### 5. Virāga and vimutti are both path-process and goal-language

SN 12.23 uses:
knowing-and-seeing → nibbidā → virāga → vimutti.

SN 43 also uses dispassion and release as names for the goal.

Therefore the same vocabulary can function:
- as process terms in a cultivation/cessation sequence,
- as epithets of the realized goal.

Do not force one fixed dictionary sense independently of context.

### 6. Iti 44: with-residue and no-residue Nibbāna elements

Iti 44 distinguishes:
- sa-upādisesa nibbānadhātu: arahant alive, faculties intact, pleasant/painful experience still possible, greed/hate/delusion extinguished;
- anupādisesa nibbānadhātu: final ending associated with no residue for future continued conditioned experience.

This strongly supports:
- liberation can be complete with respect to defilements while bodily/sensory life continues;
- final parinibbāna is not identical to the initial realization of liberation.

Text-critical guardrail:
the exact historical development and cross-tradition status of the two-element taxonomy remains to be separately audited.

### 7. SN 44.1: postmortem predicates fail at the level of the liberated Tathāgata

SN 44.1 refuses all four:
- exists after death,
- does not exist,
- both,
- neither.

The explanation is not merely “we do not know.” It says the aggregates by which one might reckon/describe the Tathāgata have been abandoned and are not subject to future arising.

Therefore the problem is partly one of applicability of the categories themselves.

This is strong evidence against treating final Nibbāna as ordinary continued existence or ordinary annihilation.

## MILINDA STRESS-TEST

### 1. No spatial storage-location — MILINDA_COMPATIBLE

The Nibbānasannihitapañha asks whether Nibbāna is stored in any direction or place.
Nāgasena says no, while maintaining that Nibbāna exists and is realized by one who practices rightly with wise attention.

The fire analogy:
fire is not stored at a fixed coordinate before manifestation; realization need not imply a spatial repository.

This is later philosophical clarification and should not be used as direct evidence that the historical Buddha used this exact analogy.

### 2. Nibbāna is not produced by kamma or causal manufacture — MILINDA_COMPATIBLE / EXTENSION

Milindapañha explicitly argues that Nibbāna is not produced in the ordinary conditioned sense and yet is cognizable/realizable by purified mind.

This is compatible with the asaṅkhata/unborn-unmade language of SN 43 and Udāna 8.3.

Caution:
Milinda makes the ontological argument more explicit and systematic than the early discourses. Treat the detailed philosophical proof as later systematization.

### 3. Reality without spatial pointing — MILINDA_EXTENSION, philosophically useful

Nāgasena uses analogies such as wind: something need not be graspable or pointable by shape/location in order to be real.

This helps explain why “if Nibbāna exists, where is it?” may contain a category mistake.

However, the analogy is a later argument. It should illuminate but not determine the early-text ontology.

### 4. The Buddha after final parinibbāna cannot be pointed to “here or there” — MILINDA_COMPATIBLE

Mil 3.5.10 uses the extinguished-fire analogy to explain why the Buddha after final parinibbāna cannot be spatially identified.

This is compatible with SN 44.1’s refusal of postmortem predicates, though the exact analogy should not be assumed to settle every metaphysical question.

## SYNTHESIS — THREE LEVELS OF NIBBĀNA LANGUAGE

### A. Functional
What ends?
- greed,
- hatred,
- delusion,
- craving,
- clinging,
- renewed becoming/birth.

This is the strongest and safest level.

### B. Process / experiential
What happens in the path?
- knowing-and-seeing,
- disenchantment,
- dispassion,
- release.

This shows how liberation is realized.

### C. Apophatic / unconditioned
How is the goal contrasted with conditioned phenomena?
- unborn,
- unbecome,
- unmade,
- unconditioned,
- deathless.

This language blocks reduction to ordinary conditioned existence, but does not license inventing a hidden eternal self.

## COUNTEREVIDENCE / GUARDRAILS

Reject:
1. “Nibbāna is merely a mood of calm.” — too narrow for asaṅkhata, amata, taṇhakkhaya and rebirth-ending language.
2. “Nibbāna is a place/heaven.” — not supported; Milinda explicitly denies fixed location.
3. “Nibbāna is a permanent soul.” — contradicted by no-self framework and postmortem non-applicability.
4. “Nibbāna is sheer annihilation.” — early texts refuse the simple ‘does not exist after death’ predicate and use positive unconditioned/deathless language.
5. “Unconditioned means created by practice.” — practice conditions realization; the goal itself is not described as fabricated by the practice.
6. “Because it is unconditioned, practice is unnecessary.” — SN 43 repeatedly gives paths leading to realization.
7. “All Nibbāna terms are exact synonyms with identical grammar/function.” — they overlap strongly, but context still matters.

## CONFIDENCE

**EBT_STRONG**
- the goal is functionally defined by ending greed/hatred/delusion;
- taṇhakkhaya, virāga, vimutti, amata, asaṅkhata and nibbāna are closely grouped as goal-language in SN 43;
- Udāna 8.3 contrasts an unconditioned escape with conditioned phenomena;
- Nibbāna can be realized while alive;
- postmortem fourfold existence predicates are not endorsed.

**TEXTUALLY STRONG IN PĀLI, CROSS-TRADITION AUDIT PENDING**
- exact two-Nibbāna-element taxonomy of Iti 44.

**MILINDA_COMPATIBLE / LATER SYSTEMATIZATION**
- no spatial location;
- not produced in the ordinary causal/fabricated sense;
- reality can be argued without spatial pointing.

**OPEN**
- full ontology of the unconditioned beyond the functions and contrasts explicitly stated;
- exact semantic history of nibbāna vs parinibbāna across early strata;
- whether every positive epithet (deathless, permanence, island, refuge, etc.) should be read literally, metaphorically, or functionally in each occurrence.

## PRACTICAL MEANING

The most text-secure way to understand Nibbāna is not to speculate first about “what substance it is,” but to ask:
- what ceases?
- what is no longer appropriated?
- what no longer conditions renewed becoming?
- what mode of knowing makes that cessation possible?

This preserves the Buddha’s recurring causal-pragmatic method.
