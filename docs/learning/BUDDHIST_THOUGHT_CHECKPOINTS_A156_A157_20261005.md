## A156 — integrity when no one is watching

Anchors: hiri-ottappa, MN 61, intention/kamma, reputation and praise/blame material.
Core:
- ethical restraint is deeper when it does not depend only on surveillance, punishment, or reputation;
- hiri is moral shame/conscience regarding what is unworthy; ottappa is moral caution regarding harmful consequences;
- MN 61 tests actions before, during, and after by harm, not by whether observers are present;
- secrecy reveals whether conduct is guided by Dhamma or image-management;
- hiri != self-hatred; moral fault can be acknowledged without permanent identity-condemnation.
Milindapanha: consulted as later/paracanonical support for virtue, consequence, and inner discipline; no override of early hiri-ottappa/MN 61 material.

## A157 — integrity and self-deception

Central question:
Why can a person know an ethical standard yet still rationalize violating it, conceal motives from themselves, or reinterpret harmful conduct as justified? How does Buddhist training expose self-deception without turning into self-hatred?

### TEXT_ATTESTED

1. MN 61 gives a direct anti-self-deception structure: examine an intended action before doing it, monitor it while doing it, and review it afterward. If harmful consequences are seen, acknowledge them, disclose where appropriate, and undertake restraint. This method interrupts the tendency to judge oneself only by prior intention or preferred self-image.
2. MN 2 shows that unwise attention can sustain taints and generate a thicket of views. A person can attend selectively to facts that protect a preferred conclusion while ignoring what reveals greed, hatred, or delusion.
3. MN 19 distinguishes lines of thought by their consequences: thoughts of sensuality, ill will, and harm lead toward affliction, while renunciation, non-ill-will, and harmlessness lead otherwise. This provides a functional test that is harder to evade through self-description.
4. MN 58 and AN 5.198 show that even truthful or corrective speech must be examined for benefit, timing, gentleness, and motive. “I am only telling the truth” is therefore not a sufficient self-justification.
5. Earlier māna, diṭṭhi-upādāna, praise/status, group identity, and reputation material shows why self-deception can protect identity: admitting error may threaten the image “I am good/right/independent/loyal.”

### SELF-DECEPTION != SIMPLE IGNORANCE

A157 distinguishes:
- ignorance: one genuinely does not know;
- rationalization: one constructs reasons after desire, fear, or identity has already tilted the decision;
- selective attention: one notices evidence supporting the preferred story while minimizing contrary evidence;
- image-protection: preserving “what kind of person I am” becomes more important than accurately seeing conduct.

These are CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS categories, not a canonical fourfold taxonomy.

### INTENTION CAN BE MIXED

A common self-deception is:
“My intention was good, therefore the action was good.”

A157 preserves:
good intention matters, but it does not cancel observable harm, unskillful means, or mixed motives.

A person can simultaneously:
- want to help;
- want praise;
- resent the recipient;
- want control.

Training therefore asks not only:
“What was my stated intention?”
but:
“What mental factors were actually present?”

### MN 61 — REVIEW BREAKS IMAGE-PROTECTION

The before/during/after structure is powerful because it makes revision normal.

If harm appears:
- stop or correct where possible;
- acknowledge rather than defend;
- learn for the future.

This creates an alternative to two extremes:
- deny error to protect identity;
- collapse into “I am a bad person.”

The middle is:
“this action was harmful; it must be corrected.”

### RATIONALIZATION OF ANGER

Example:
“I am harsh because truth matters.”

Possible hidden factors:
- anger;
- humiliation;
- superiority;
- wish to win.

MN 58/AN 5.198 give a test:
if the same truth could be conveyed more beneficially, at a better time, without hostility, then harshness cannot be excused merely by truth-content.

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION.

### RATIONALIZATION OF ATTACHMENT

Example:
“I am only caring for them.”

Possible hidden factors:
- fear of losing them;
- need for gratitude;
- control;
- reflected identity.

A138–A142 guardrails remain active:
care must be distinguished from possession, and goodwill from right view.

### RATIONALIZATION OF GROUP LOYALTY

Example:
“I am protecting the community.”

Possible hidden factor:
protecting reputation rather than truth or welfare.

A150–A155 provide the diagnostic:
Does the action protect beings and Dhamma, or primarily preserve group identity/status?

### WHY SELF-DECEPTION IS ATTRACTIVE

A157's synthesis:
accurate seeing can produce:
- shame about an action;
- loss of status;
- need to apologize;
- need to change;
- uncertainty.

Rationalization reduces that immediate discomfort by preserving the existing self-story.

Thus self-deception can function as short-term relief purchased at the cost of longer-term ignorance.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### INTEGRITY = WILLINGNESS TO UPDATE

A157 proposes a guarded working definition:
integrity is not “never being wrong.”
It is the capacity to let evidence about one's own conduct revise one's story, choices, and future restraint.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### PRACTICAL DIAGNOSTIC

When defending one's own action, ask:
1. What did I actually do?
2. What harm or benefit followed?
3. What intention did I claim?
4. What other motives may also have been present?
5. What evidence am I minimizing because it threatens my self-image?
6. Would I judge the same behavior differently if another person did it?
7. Am I using truth, loyalty, compassion, courage, or patience as a cover-word?
8. What would I have to admit if I stopped defending myself?
9. What repair or restraint would follow from that admission?
10. Can I acknowledge the action as wrong without turning it into “I am permanently bad”?

### MILINDAPANHA — MANDATORY

Consulted: YES.

Milindapanha provides later/paracanonical support through its repeated method of exposing hidden assumptions in a question, splitting conflated cases, and refusing answers that depend on equivocation.

Role:
later support for anti-rationalization through distinction-making and examination.

Guardrail:
Milindapanha does not replace MN 61, MN 2, MN 19, MN 58, or AN 5.198.

### CORRECTIONS PRESERVED

- self-deception != simple ignorance;
- good intention != guaranteed skillfulness;
- mixed motive != total falsity of every good intention;
- admitting fault != self-hatred;
- integrity != never being wrong;
- truth-content != license for hostility;
- loyalty != concealment;
- compassion != control;
- courage != refusal to admit error;
- Milindapanha remains later/paracanonical support.
