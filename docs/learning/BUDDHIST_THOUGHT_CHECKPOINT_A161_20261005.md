## Completed checkpoint A161

## Central question

If the five aggregates are not-self, why do early discourses still speak of a "person" or "being", and how can this be understood without falling into either a permanent-self doctrine or annihilationism/nihilism?

A161 is primarily an integration checkpoint. It incorporates the merged SN 22 ID-level audit while preserving the fact that the SN 22 audit lane remains open.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. SN 22.22 — the burden and the burden-carrier

SN 22.22 distinguishes:

- the burden: the five aggregates subject to clinging;
- the burden-carrier: the `puggala` / person, described by name and clan;
- taking up the burden: craving that makes for renewed existence;
- laying down the burden: the fading, cessation, relinquishment, release, and non-reliance regarding that craving.

This is important because the discourse itself uses ordinary person-language while analyzing suffering through the five clinging-aggregates and craving.

**Text-attested limit:** SN 22.22 does not in this passage give a complete metaphysical definition of `puggala`. The word must not be silently converted either into a permanent essence or into a later reductionist theory.

### 2. SN 5.10 — the chariot simile and conventional "being" language

In SN 5.10, Vajirā rejects Māra's reification of a "being". The discourse compares a chariot, named on the basis of an assemblage of parts, with the use of the designation "being" when the aggregates are present.

This gives an early-discourse basis for saying:

- ordinary person/being language can function;
- the name does not establish a separately findable permanent essence;
- analysis returns attention to conditioned aggregates and suffering rather than a metaphysical entity hidden behind them.

The exact wording varies by translation, so A161 does not promote one English term such as "convention", "designation", or "name" into an exclusive lexical equivalent without further lexical work.

### 3. SN 22.59 — not-self is applied to each aggregate

SN 22.59 directly says that form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are not-self.

Its reasoning includes:

- if an aggregate were self, it would not be liable to affliction in the stated way;
- one would be able to command it, "let it be thus / let it not be thus";
- what is impermanent, stressful, and subject to change is not fit to be regarded as "this is mine / I am this / this is my self";
- right seeing leads to disenchantment, dispassion, and release.

**Guardrail:** SN 22.59 does not say "nothing exists". Its task is to undermine appropriation and self-identification with the aggregates.

### 4. SN 22.48 — five aggregates and five clinging-aggregates are not identical expressions

SN 22.48 explicitly teaches both the five aggregates and the five aggregates subject to clinging.

Therefore A161 preserves the merged audit correction:

`pañcakkhandha` and `pañcupādānakkhandha` must not be flattened into one expression in every context.

This matters for person-language because the analysis concerns both the occurrence of aggregate processes and the additional issue of appropriation/clinging.

### 5. SN 22.89 — identity-view and the residual "I am" are distinguishable

Khemaka says that he does not regard any of the five clinging-aggregates as self or belonging to self, yet a residual "I am" conceit/desire/tendency remains until further contemplation of arising and passing removes it.

This directly blocks the simplification that all forms of self-reference or selfing are one single phenomenon.

At minimum, the discourse distinguishes:

- explicit self-identification around the aggregates;
- a subtler residual "I am".

A161 does not equate either of these automatically with ordinary grammatical use of "I".

### 6. SN 22.95 — empty/void/without substance does not mean "nonexistent"

SN 22.95 compares:

- form to foam;
- feeling to a bubble;
- perception to a mirage;
- formations to a banana trunk;
- consciousness to a magic trick.

On examination they are described as empty, void, and without substance, followed by disenchantment, dispassion, and release.

**Audit guardrail:** this does not textually license the claim "the aggregates do not exist". The similes undermine substance/reliability/appropriation, not ordinary empirical occurrence.

### 7. SN 22.85 — annihilationism is explicitly rejected as a wrong view

SN 22.85 presents Yamaka holding the view that an arahant is annihilated, perishes, and does not exist after death.

That view is explicitly corrected.

Therefore the move:

`anattā` -> "the enlightened person is simply annihilated into nothing"

is not an acceptable reading of the early evidence used here.

### 8. SN 22.86 — the Tathāgata cannot be pinned down by the four postmortem alternatives

SN 22.86 tests whether the Tathāgata can be identified as:

- any aggregate;
- in an aggregate;
- apart from an aggregate;
- all aggregates together;
- something without the aggregates.

The answers are negative. The discourse then rejects the attempt to state the Tathāgata after death through the four alternatives of existence, non-existence, both, or neither.

A161 uses this only as an anti-annihilation / anti-reification guardrail. The full postmortem question-method was studied earlier in A120.

### 9. SN 12.15 — Right View avoids the existence/non-existence duality

SN 12.15 says the world largely relies on the duality of existence and non-existence.

Seeing arising and cessation with right wisdom, without grasping and fixing on "my self", avoids these extremes and is presented through dependent arising.

This supplies a broader guardrail for A161:

not-self should not be converted into either a doctrine of a permanent entity or a doctrine that reality is simply nothing.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. "Person" can function without a permanent self-essence

Across SN 22.22 and SN 5.10, the most defensible synthesis is:

**ordinary person-language can be used for a conditioned aggregate process without requiring the speaker to posit a permanent, independently findable self-substance.**

This is a cross-text formulation, not one verbatim canonical sentence.

It preserves two facts at once:

1. early discourses do use terms such as person/being;
2. they reject identifying the aggregates as self and challenge reification of a being.

### B. Not-self is not the same proposition as "nothing exists"

A161 distinguishes:

- **not-self analysis:** these conditioned aggregates are not fit to be taken as "mine / I / my self";
- **nihilistic overreach:** nothing exists, no person-language is meaningful, action and suffering are unreal.

The first is strongly text-attested in the cited discourses.
The second is not supported by them and is directly constrained by SN 22.85, SN 22.86, and SN 12.15.

### C. Two opposite errors

A161 identifies two opposed interpretive errors.

#### Reification

A process or designation is turned into a permanent inner owner/controller:
"there must be a fixed entity behind the aggregates."

#### Annihilation

Because no fixed self can be found in the aggregates, one concludes:
"therefore there is literally nothing here and liberation is destruction of an entity."

The early evidence blocks both moves.

This two-error framing is CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS.

### D. The practical direction is de-appropriation

The recurring early move is not to solve an abstract puzzle about what a person "really is" in a final metaphysical sense.

It repeatedly asks whether conditioned experience should be appropriated as:

- mine;
- I;
- my self.

Then it tracks the consequences of relinquishing that appropriation:

- disenchantment;
- dispassion;
- release.

Thus A161's practical formulation is:

**the teaching turns attention from defending an essence to understanding conditioned suffering and ceasing appropriation.**

This is synthesis grounded in SN 22.59, SN 5.10, SN 12.15, and related SN 22 passages.

### E. Self-view, residual "I am", and ordinary speech must not be collapsed

SN 22.89 shows that absence of explicit self-view can coexist with a residual "I am".

SN 5.10 and SN 22.22 show that person/being language continues to function in discourse.

Therefore A161 keeps three levels analytically separate:

1. explicit self-view / appropriation;
2. residual `asmimāna` or "I am" conceit;
3. ordinary conventional first-person/person-language.

This three-level model is CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS. The texts do not present it as one numbered taxonomy.

## Practical understanding

A161's working diagnostic is:

When the thought "Who am I really?" becomes rigid, ask:

1. What present aggregate/process is actually being observed?
2. What part is being converted into "mine", "I", or "my self"?
3. Is a useful name being mistaken for a permanent essence?
4. Am I reacting to the absence of a fixed essence by jumping to "nothing exists"?
5. What suffering is produced by grasping this identity?
6. What changes if the process is known without appropriation?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical list.
