## Central question

How can a person accept full responsibility for harmful action without converting that action into a fixed self-identity?

A171 distinguishes:
- "I did this harmful action";
- "this action had these consequences";
- "I am responsible for restraint and repair";
from:
- "therefore I am essentially and permanently a bad self."

The first three can support moral clarity.
The last move is a further identity construction.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. MN 61 — wrongdoing is handled through examination, acknowledgment, and restraint

MN 61 repeatedly asks whether an action:
- harms oneself;
- harms others;
- harms both.

If harmful bodily or verbal action has already occurred, the response is not metaphysical self-condemnation.

The training is:
- recognize the action as harmful;
- acknowledge/disclose it;
- restrain oneself in the future.

Thus the early-text emphasis is behavioral and causal:
**what was done, what harm followed, and what changes now.**

### 2. SN 42.8 — self-consuming remorse does not undo the past

SN 42.8 explicitly rejects getting consumed by remorse over past harmful deeds as though remorse could make them not have happened.

The constructive direction is:
- understand that the past cannot be undone;
- undertake restraint for the future;
- cultivate a different way of living.

This strongly guards against:
`more self-punishment = more moral repair`.

### 3. DN 2 — recognizing transgression supports growth

DN 2 contains the recurring pattern:
- recognize a transgression as transgression;
- acknowledge it;
- undertake future restraint;
- thereby grow in the discipline.

The text does not require:
- denying the wrong;
- minimizing it;
- or building a permanent identity around it.

### 4. AN 5.57 — ownership of action is responsibility, not an eternal essence

AN 5.57 teaches recollection that beings are owners/heirs of their actions.

A171 uses this carefully:
action has consequence and responsibility matters.

But the statement is about causal ownership of action, not an assertion that one harmful action reveals an eternal, unchangeable moral essence.

The anti-essentialist extension is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### 5. SN 22.59 — aggregates are not to be appropriated as self

SN 22.59 instructs that form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are to be seen as:
- not mine;
- not I;
- not my self.

Applied cautiously to moral self-condemnation:
even painful mental states, memories, and remorse are not grounds for constructing a permanent self.

The specific application to guilt identity is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### 6. SN 22.89 — residual "I am" can persist after explicit self-view is corrected

SN 22.89 distinguishes explicit identity-view from the subtler residual "I am" conceit.

A171 therefore adds a further warning:
a person can abandon one identity-story ("I am righteous") and replace it with another ("I am irredeemably bad").

The exact guilt application is synthesis; the self-view vs residual "I am" distinction is text-attested.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Responsibility and identity must be separated

A171 distinguishes:

1. **action:** what was done;
2. **consequence:** what harm followed;
3. **responsibility:** what one must acknowledge, repair, or restrain;
4. **identity claim:** "this is what I essentially am."

The first three may be necessary for moral clarity.
The fourth is not required for accountability.

### B. Self-condemnation can become another form of self-preoccupation

A person can appear morally serious while remaining centered on:
- "how terrible I am";
- "what kind of person this makes me";
- "whether I can ever feel clean again."

This can shift attention away from:
- the harmed person;
- restitution;
- future restraint;
- concrete reform.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### C. Remorse is useful only insofar as it supports learning and restraint

A171 does not reject all remorse.

Useful remorse:
- helps recognition;
- interrupts repetition;
- motivates confession/amends;
- strengthens future restraint.

Unhelpful remorse:
- becomes repetitive self-attack;
- produces paralysis;
- demands reassurance;
- substitutes suffering-for-self for repair-of-harm.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS from MN 61, SN 42.8, A131, A158, and A169.

### D. "I did evil" is more precise than "I am evil"

The formulation:
`I did a harmful action`
keeps moral evaluation attached to conduct.

The formulation:
`I am essentially evil`
turns conduct into identity.

A171 treats the first as compatible with strong accountability and the second as an overextension.

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical quote.

### E. Non-self does not erase responsibility

A171 explicitly rejects:
`not-self -> no one is responsible`.

Previous checkpoints already established:
- conventional person-language remains valid;
- kamma/intention and responsibility remain meaningful;
- non-self blocks permanent ownership/essence, not causal accountability.

Thus:
**no fixed self does not mean no consequences.**

### F. Reform requires neither innocence fiction nor permanent stain

Two errors are rejected:

- **self-absolution:** "there is no self, so what I did does not matter";
- **self-condemnation:** "what I did proves what I eternally am."

The middle direction is:
- face the act fully;
- face consequences;
- repair what can be repaired;
- train future conduct;
- do not turn the act into a metaphysical identity.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. "Self-condemnation", "guilt identity", and "moral essence" are modern analytic terms.
2. Early Pāli vocabulary for remorse, shame, conscience, and regret requires separate lexical control: `kukkucca`, `hiri`, `ottappa`, etc. must not be collapsed.
3. Healthy hiri/ottappa should not be equated with neurotic self-hatred.
4. SN 22.59 and SN 22.89 do not directly discuss modern guilt psychology; that application remains synthesis.
5. Some severe wrongdoing may require long-lasting consequences even after genuine reform.
6. Non-self must never be used to evade restitution, law, discipline, or safety boundaries.

## Practical understanding

After serious wrongdoing, ask:

1. What exactly did I do?
2. Who was harmed and how?
3. What responsibility is mine?
4. What can still be repaired?
5. What future restraint is required?
6. What consequence must I accept?
7. Am I facing the harm, or mainly attacking myself?
8. Is self-hatred helping the harmed person in any concrete way?
9. Am I trying to escape responsibility by saying "there is no self"?
10. Can I hold the sentence "I did wrong" without converting it into "I am permanently wrong"?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical ten-step list.
