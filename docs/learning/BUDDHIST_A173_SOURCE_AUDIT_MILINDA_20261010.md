## 2. TEXT_ATTESTED — EARLY DISCOURSE AXIS

### AN 2.9, Lokapāla: hiri and ottappa

The discourse identifies hiri and ottappa as two bright/wholesome qualities protecting the world. English translations differ (conscience/concern, shame/fear of wrongdoing). The passage does NOT supply a complete psychological taxonomy of anxiety, physiological alarm, or courage.

Source: https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/accesstoinsight/html/tipitaka/an/an02/an02.009.irel.html

### MN 61, advice to Rāhula

Actions of body, speech, and mind are examined before, during, and after action for harm to oneself, others, or both. The treatment of recognized harmful actions includes disclosure to a teacher or sensible companion where applicable and future restraint. Consequence-awareness is part of careful ethical evaluation; the discourse does not quantify risks in modern probability language.

Source: https://suttacentral.net/mn61/en/sujato
Cross-readable translation: https://dailydharmapractice.com/suttas/ambalatthika-rahulovada-sutta

### MN 4, Bhayabherava

In a specific pre-awakening wilderness context, the bodhisatta describes examining unpurified conduct and fear, then facing fear in the posture in which it arose instead of automatically switching posture to escape the experience. This is evidence about cultivation of mind in that setting; it is NOT a generic instruction to remain physically exposed to a dangerous animal, violent person, or unsafe situation.

Source: https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN4.html
Parallel translation: https://suttacentral.net/mn4/en/suddhaso

### Vinaya: Nālāgiri episode, Pi Tv Kd 17

In an earlier disciplinary narrative, the Buddha is accompanied by several monks; the monks urge him to turn back when the dangerous elephant approaches. The text consulted does NOT say that exactly five hundred arahants fled, nor does it attribute the later Milindapañha explanatory motive to them.

Source: https://dhammatalks.net/suttacentral/sc2016/sc/en/pi-tv-kd17.html

## 3. LATER/PARACANONICAL — MANDATORY MILINDAPAÑHA CONSULTATION

**Mi Tiên Vấn Đáp / Milindapañha 5.4.9 (On the Fearlessness of the Arahats): CONSULTED, exact locus checked.**

King Milinda poses an apparent contradiction: arahants are said to be free from fear, yet a narrative says five hundred arahants withdrew before a charging elephant while Ānanda remained. Nāgasena answers that their withdrawal was motivated by anticipated benefits, rather than internal trembling/fear. The dialectical distinction is between a visible action and its inferred psychological cause.

Source: https://dhammatalks.net/suttacentral/sc2016/sc/en/mil5.4.9.html
Alternative edition and translator notes: https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/milindapanha-questions-of-king-milinda/d/doc1460835.html

**Critical source discrepancy:** the translator's footnote and direct Vinaya cross-check show that the earlier Vinaya story calls the elephant Nālāgiri and does not present the five-hundred-arahants-fleeing scene. Therefore:
- Do not promote the Milindapañha narrative particulars to early-discourse historical fact.
- Do not falsely attribute Nāgasena's account of *why* those figures withdrew to the early Vinaya.
- Mil 5.4.9 illustrates that withdrawal does not logically establish mental fear; it does NOT expressly teach that they withdrew *for ordinary personal safety*. An unqualified earlier draft reference to “prudent withdrawal” must be narrowed to this actually described rationale.
- This is later explanatory material compatible with an ethical distinction, not a proof of the mental state of historical people.

Optional related Milindapañha passage for future checking: Mil 5.2.3 on fear of death and the arahant exception. It was located, not required to infer psychological facts beyond its stated narrative.

## 4. CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS — WHAT THIS PASS ADDS

**Bounded thesis:** The mere physical act of withdrawing cannot by itself prove inner cowardice; and the mere physical act of confronting danger cannot by itself prove wisdom.

Three distinct lenses:
1. Moral criterion: is an intended action unwholesome/harmful? (AN 2.9, MN 61)
2. Perception and mind training: is fear being observed or unconsciously governing conduct? (MN 4, in its limited context)
3. Action and motive: what conditions and reasons explain a visible retreat or engagement? (Mil 5.4.9, later argumentative illustration)

Do NOT conflate these with one-to-one lexical equations. Ottappa does not equal generalized anxiety; bhaya/bherava are not simply synonyms for ottappa. “Fear of losing face” and “cowardice” are analytical modern labels, not exact early Pāli quotations.

**Correction to A173 DRAFT:** Its broad Milindapañha line about “prudent withdrawal” can overstate this specific Mil 5.4.9 exchange. In this exchange, the stated motive is the anticipated illustrative/religious benefit of withdrawing. A modern safety-based retreat example is an APPLICATION/SYNTHESIS, not text-attested Mil 5.4.9 content.

## 5. APPLICATION TEST — PROPOSED, NOT AN OUTCOME MEASUREMENT

Case A: A person faces a credible threat while considering whether to report an ethical breach. They collect evidence and use a safer reporting route instead of confronting the aggressor immediately.
- Working reading: may combine moral restraint, consequence-awareness and prudent preparation; declining immediate confrontation alone does not show cowardice.
- Boundary: requires context; the sources do not certify this particular real-world decision as correct.

Case B: A person loudly confronts someone mainly to avoid looking weak, while increasing danger to others.
- Working reading: external boldness does not establish wholesome courage. Apply MN 61's harm-review lens.
- Boundary: intent and consequence require evidence, not confident guessing from appearance.

Case C: A frightened meditator notices fear arising in a safe practice setting without immediately fleeing the sensation.
- Working reading: a cautious analogy to MN 4's training in its own context.
- Boundary: NOT advised in actual physical danger; no diagnosis or medical claim.

Mini assessment: If two observers see exactly the same retreat, can they reliably infer identical inner fear? **No.** Source of that reasoning method: later Mil 5.4.9; connection to early moral discernment is CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS, not direct early-text attestation.
