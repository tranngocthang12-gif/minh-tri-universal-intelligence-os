## Completed checkpoint A169

## Central question

What evidence should count as genuine moral change after wrongdoing?

A169 distinguishes:
- saying sorry;
- feeling remorse;
- acknowledging responsibility;
- accepting consequences;
- restraining future conduct;
- demonstrating changed behavior over time.

These are not automatically equivalent.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. MN 61 — wrongdoing should produce acknowledgment and future restraint

MN 61 is central.

When a bodily or verbal action is recognized as harmful, the response includes:
- acknowledgment/disclosure;
- reflection;
- future restraint.

Therefore:

**recognizing wrong without changing future conduct is incomplete according to the training logic of MN 61.**

### 2. MN 65 — the response to correction is itself evidence

MN 65 distinguishes practitioners by patterns such as:
- recurrence of offenses;
- evasiveness;
- anger or bitterness when corrected;
- whether one changes one's ways.

Therefore:
how a person responds when corrected is part of the evidence about reform.

A smooth apology after the event does not erase repeated defensiveness during correction.

### 3. DN 2 — growth is associated with recognizing transgression as transgression

DN 2 includes the recurring early Buddhist pattern that recognizing a transgression as transgression, making amends/confessing appropriately, and undertaking restraint is a condition for growth in the discipline.

A169 uses the bounded result:

**moral growth begins with accurate recognition of wrongdoing, not euphemism or blame reversal.**

### 4. SN 42.8 — the past cannot be undone by remorse alone

SN 42.8 warns against simply consuming oneself with remorse over past killing and wrong actions; instead one should recognize the past cannot be undone and undertake restraint for the future.

This is especially important:

**pain about the past is not the same as reform.**

The future pattern matters.

### 5. AN 4.192 — stable character is known over time

AN 4.192 teaches that qualities such as virtue and discernment are known through sustained observation and relevant testing over time.

A169 therefore rejects:

`short period of good behavior = established character change`.

### 6. MN 47 — durable observable qualities matter

MN 47's investigation of the teacher emphasizes observable qualities and whether they are long-standing rather than merely recent.

Applied cautiously to moral reform:

**lasting conduct is stronger evidence than a brief post-crisis performance.**

This application is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### 7. AN 5.57 — ownership of action preserves responsibility

AN 5.57's recollection that beings are owners/heirs of their actions supports responsibility without requiring permanent condemnation.

A person is answerable for action, but action patterns can also change through new choices.

A169 uses this only as a responsibility guardrail.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. A169 distinguishes six layers of "change"

1. **verbal apology** — saying one is sorry;
2. **affective remorse** — feeling pain, shame, regret, or fear;
3. **cognitive acknowledgment** — clearly naming what was wrong;
4. **responsibility acceptance** — no blame-shifting, minimization, or excuse as substitute;
5. **future restraint** — concrete reduction/non-repetition of harmful action;
6. **stable reform** — new conduct persists across time and relevant situations.

This six-layer model is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS, not a canonical list.

### B. Remorse can be useful but is not sufficient

Remorse may help if it supports:
- recognition;
- confession;
- repair;
- restraint.

It becomes unhelpful when it turns into:
- self-consuming guilt;
- paralysis;
- image-management;
- demand for immediate forgiveness;
- repeated emotional display without behavioral change.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS grounded in MN 61, SN 42.8, and prior A131/A158.

### C. Genuine change reduces recurrence

The strongest practical signal of reform is not how intensely a person speaks about change but whether the harmful behavior actually becomes less likely and less frequent.

This is a synthesis from:
- MN 61 future restraint;
- MN 65 recurrence and response to correction;
- AN 4.192 long observation;
- MN 47 stable qualities.

### D. Acceptance of consequence is part of evidence

If a person claims to have changed but:
- refuses reasonable consequences;
- demands instant restoration of trust;
- attacks anyone who recalls the harm;
- treats accountability as persecution,

then the claim of reform remains weakly supported.

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION.

No single early discourse states this modern checklist.

### E. Reform is not metaphysical identity replacement

A169 rejects:
- "once bad, always bad";
- "one apology makes everything new."

Early Buddhist causal thinking allows habits and intentions to change, but evidence must track actual causal change in conduct.

This is synthesis, not a direct canonical slogan.

### F. Genuine change should become less self-centered

A subtle diagnostic:

If repentance remains focused mainly on:
- "do people still think I am bad?"
- "when will I get my status back?"
- "why haven't they forgiven me?"

then identity/status may still dominate.

More mature reform increasingly centers:
- harm done;
- responsibility;
- restraint;
- repair;
- reduction of future harm.

This is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. No early discourse here gives a universal time period after which reform is proven.
2. No single apology formula guarantees change.
3. Severity and type of wrongdoing matter.
4. Some contexts may require restitution or institutional process not fully covered by the sources used here.
5. MN 47 concerns examination of the Tathāgata/teacher; post-harm reform application remains synthesis.
6. AN 4.192 gives observation-over-time logic but not a fixed trust-restoration rule.
7. Full Vinaya audit is still needed for stronger claims about formal rehabilitation after disciplinary offenses.

## Practical understanding

When evaluating claimed reform, ask:

1. Did the person clearly name what they did wrong?
2. Did they stop blaming others for their own action?
3. Did they accept reasonable consequence?
4. Did they make concrete changes that reduce recurrence?
5. What happened the next time the same trigger appeared?
6. How did they respond when corrected again?
7. Has the changed behavior persisted over time?
8. Is the person focused on reducing harm, or mainly restoring their image?
9. Is trust being requested faster than evidence is accumulating?
10. What evidence would count against the claim of reform?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical ten-step list.
