**Topic:** Strength without domination — distinguish firmness, authority, control, coercion, and the need to dominate others.

## Central question

How can a person exercise strength, authority, or control where functionally necessary without converting power into superiority, coercive identity, or domination?

A175 uses modern umbrella terms such as "domination" and "coercion" cautiously. It does not claim a one-to-one mapping to a single early Pāli technical term.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. Snp 4.9 — comparative self-positioning feeds dispute

Snp 4.9 directly connects conceiving oneself as equal, superior, or inferior with dispute.

This gives A175 a key guardrail:
power becomes spiritually dangerous when it is organized around comparative self-positioning rather than function.

### 2. Snp 4.14 — knowledge and position should not become rank identity

Snp 4.14 again warns against conceiving oneself as superior, inferior, or equal on the basis of what one knows.

A175 extends this carefully:
authority or competence should not become evidence that "I am a higher kind of person."

The authority application is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### 3. MN 58 / AN 5.198 — correction remains constrained by truth, benefit, timing, and goodwill

Even when one has authority to correct another, the correction is not ethically free-form.

Early material constrains speech and admonition by:
- truth;
- benefit;
- timing;
- gentleness;
- goodwill rather than hatred.

Therefore:
**having power to correct does not create permission to humiliate.**

### 4. MN 103–104 — order and dispute-resolution can exist without factional hostility

MN 103 analyzes defilements that intensify dispute.
MN 104 preserves procedural approaches for maintaining concord and resolving conflict.

This supports:
- structure;
- rules;
- correction;
- resolution procedures;

without requiring domination as identity.

### 5. MN 65 — discipline can be differentiated by conduct and response

MN 65 shows that disciplinary response can vary according to:
- recurrence;
- evasiveness;
- anger at correction;
- actual reform.

Thus authority may need to impose different consequences without making the authority-holder morally superior.

Guardrail:
this is a monastic context.

### 6. SN 22.89 — "I am" conceit can survive explicit doctrinal correction

SN 22.89 provides the recurring guardrail:
even when explicit self-view is weakened, subtle identity can remain.

Applied to power:
one can move from "I possess authority" to "I am the powerful one."

The application is synthesis.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Five distinct things should be separated

A175 distinguishes:

1. **capacity** — ability to act or influence outcomes;
2. **authority** — legitimate role-based permission/responsibility;
3. **control** — limiting or directing behavior for a defined function;
4. **coercion** — overriding another's choice through pressure/threat/force;
5. **domination identity** — needing others to submit in order to affirm one's status or self.

These are not synonyms.

### B. Firmness does not require humiliation

A boundary can be clear.
A consequence can be real.
An instruction can be non-negotiable.

None of these logically requires:
- contempt;
- insult;
- degradation;
- proving superiority.

Thus:
**firmness and domination are separable.**

### C. Functional authority should be proportionate to the task

A175 proposes a guarded synthesis:
authority should be tied to:
- protection;
- coordination;
- accountability;
- safety;
- practice integrity;

not to personal gratification from being obeyed.

### D. Domination reveals itself when disobedience becomes an identity threat

A useful diagnostic:
if disagreement or non-compliance produces not only concern about function, but also:
- humiliation;
- rage at "disrespect";
- need to break resistance;
- need to reassert rank;

then identity/status may be driving the response.

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION.

### E. Strength can include restraint

Power is not only:
- pushing;
- forcing;
- speaking louder;
- punishing.

It can include:
- not retaliating;
- not humiliating;
- using only necessary force;
- stopping escalation;
- delegating;
- withdrawing;
- accepting limits.

### F. Authority without ego is more correctable

If authority is treated as a function rather than self-worth, then correction of the authority-holder is less threatening.

This links A165:
corrigibility becomes easier when "being in charge" is not part of a defended identity.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. "Domination", "coercion", and "authority" are modern analytic categories.
2. No single early discourse here supplies a complete political theory of legitimate power.
3. MN 65 and MN 104 are monastic/community contexts; state/political extension remains guarded.
4. Necessary coercion in emergencies may differ ethically from domination-as-identity.
5. Full Vinaya audit is needed before stronger institutional claims.
6. `māna`, `maññati`, and `asmimāna` must remain lexically distinct.

## Practical understanding

When exercising authority, ask:

1. What function am I responsible for?
2. What minimum control is actually necessary?
3. Am I correcting behavior or defending status?
4. Would I choose the same response if nobody were impressed by it?
5. Does this consequence protect or merely humiliate?
6. Is stronger force justified by greater risk, or by my anger?
7. Can I accept criticism without feeling my authority has collapsed?
8. Can I preserve the boundary without needing the other person to feel inferior?
9. Can I stop once the functional goal is achieved?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical nine-step list.
