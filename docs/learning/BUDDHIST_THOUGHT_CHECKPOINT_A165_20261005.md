## Completed checkpoint A165

## Central question

How can a person hold a view strongly, act decisively, and still remain corrigible when better evidence or clearer seeing appears?

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. MN 95 — preserve truth before claiming full possession of truth

MN 95 distinguishes the grounds on which one may believe something from actual realization.

Its discipline is important:
- one may honestly say "this is my faith", "this is my tradition", "this is what reasoning supports", or "this is the view I have accepted";
- but one should not convert those grounds prematurely into the absolute claim that only this is true and everything else is false.

This makes corrigibility part of truthfulness:
one does not weaken conviction; one reports its epistemic basis accurately.

### 2. MN 47 — the teacher may be investigated rather than merely trusted

MN 47 instructs a capable disciple to examine the Tathāgata with respect to observable qualities and the stability of those qualities.

This provides direct early support for:
- scrutiny rather than blind loyalty;
- checking conduct over time;
- confidence that is strengthened by examination rather than protected from examination.

Therefore:
**respect and investigation are not opposites.**

### 3. AN 3.65/66 — authority, tradition, hearsay, and reasoning are not sufficient by themselves

The Kesamutti/Kālāma material warns against accepting a claim merely because it comes from:
- oral tradition;
- lineage;
- hearsay;
- a collection of texts;
- abstract reasoning;
- inferential reasoning;
- reflective consideration;
- agreement with one's preconceptions;
- a speaker's competence;
- reverence for a teacher.

The discourse then turns to knowing for oneself whether qualities are unwholesome, blameworthy, criticized by the wise, and conducive to harm/suffering, versus their opposites.

A165 therefore preserves:
**confidence should be testable against conduct and consequences, not insulated by authority alone.**

### 4. MN 58 — truth alone is not enough for speech

MN 58 distinguishes speech by whether it is:
- true or false;
- beneficial or harmful;
- pleasing or displeasing;
- timely or untimely.

The Buddha does not justify speech merely with "it is true".

This matters for intellectual humility:
being correct does not exempt one from examining motive, benefit, timing, and compassion.

### 5. MN 61 — correction requires acknowledgment and future restraint

MN 61 teaches review of action before, during, and after.

When harmful action is recognized, the relevant response includes:
- acknowledgment;
- disclosure where appropriate;
- future restraint;
- training.

This gives a behavioral model of corrigibility:
recognizing error should change future action, not merely produce self-description.

### 6. SN 22.89 — doctrinal correction does not automatically remove residual conceit

Khemaka has abandoned explicit identity-view regarding the aggregates but still reports a residual "I am" conceit/tendency.

Applied cautiously to A165:
a person can correct the proposition while still being attached to the identity of "the one who is right."

The identity application is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS; the self-view vs residual "I am" distinction itself is text-attested.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Strong confidence and corrigibility are compatible

A165 distinguishes:

1. **weak uncertainty** — no basis, no commitment;
2. **grounded confidence** — commitment proportional to evidence and practice;
3. **dogmatic possession** — commitment tied to identity, status, or immunity from correction.

The project rejects the equation:
`confidence = dogmatism`.

It also rejects:
`humility = never making a firm claim`.

### B. Corrigibility requires two things

A person must be able to:
- change the proposition when evidence changes;
- loosen identity attachment so that correction does not feel like annihilation of self-worth.

The first is epistemic.
The second concerns conceit/appropriation.

This distinction is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### C. A useful test: what happens when counterevidence appears?

If new evidence appears, observe:

- Does attention move toward examination?
- Or toward protecting reputation?
- Does one ask "is this true?"
- Or "how do I avoid being shown wrong?"
- Can one acknowledge an error and alter conduct?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION drawing on MN 95, MN 47, MN 61, and prior conceit material.

### D. Being right does not license contempt

Snp comparison material and right-speech material together support a guardrail:

Even if one's factual or doctrinal conclusion is correct, superiority, humiliation of others, and identity-ranking are separate mental events.

Correctness does not make contempt wholesome.

### E. Correction is not self-negation

Acknowledging an error does not require:
- declaring oneself worthless;
- abandoning all confidence;
- distrusting every future judgment.

MN 61's model is procedural:
see the harmful action clearly, acknowledge it, restrain it, continue training.

Thus:
**correction is a change in understanding/action, not a ritual destruction of identity.**

## LATER/PARACANONICAL — MILINDAPAÑHA

Milindapañha repeatedly uses question-answer, objection, distinction, analogy, and counterexample.

Its value for A165 is methodological:
- a claim may be challenged without disrespect;
- apparent contradictions may be decomposed;
- reasoning is useful but not identical with liberating wisdom.

It supplies a model of disciplined disputation, not an override of early-text standards.

1. AN numbering differs across editions; Kālāma/Kesamutti references must be normalized in any future lexical/source audit.

3. MN 47 is about examining the Tathāgata/teacher; extending its logic to every claim or institution is synthesis.
4. SN 22.89 does not specifically discuss intellectual pride; that application remains synthesis.
5. Strong confidence after direct realization may differ from ordinary evidential confidence; do not collapse them.

## Practical understanding

When I strongly believe I am right:

1. What is the basis of this confidence?
2. Am I describing that basis accurately?
3. What evidence would make me revise the claim?
4. Have I actually looked for counterevidence?
5. Am I protecting truth, or protecting my status as the person who knows?
6. If corrected, can I state the correction plainly?
7. Does the correction change future behavior?
8. Can I remain firm where evidence is strong without despising people who disagree?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical eight-step list.
