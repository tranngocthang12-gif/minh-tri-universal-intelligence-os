## Central question

Can harmful conduct be stopped, consequences imposed, and offenders held responsible without turning correction into hatred or revenge?

A167 uses "justice" only as a modern umbrella label. It does **not** claim that the early discourses present one technical category exactly equivalent to a modern theory of justice.

## TEXT_ATTESTED

### 1. Dhammapada 3–5 — hostility is not ended by hostility

The early verse sequence explicitly contrasts brooding on injury with relinquishing hostility and says hostility is not stilled through hostility but through non-hostility.

This directly blocks revenge as a spiritual cure for injury.

Text-attested limit:
the verses do not provide a complete social/legal theory of punishment.

### 2. AN 5.161 — resentment can be removed without denying kamma/accountability

AN 5.161 gives several methods for removing hatred toward a person, including:
- goodwill;
- compassion;
- equanimity;
- non-attention;
- recollection that the person is owner/heir of their actions.

This is especially important for A167 because non-hatred and moral consequence appear together.

The text does not say:
"because I should not hate them, their actions have no consequences."

### 3. AN 5.162 — compassion can be directed precisely toward someone behaving badly

AN 5.162 discusses a person impure in bodily and verbal conduct and says one should arouse compassion and wish that this person abandon wrong conduct and develop right conduct.

This supports:

**compassion for the wrongdoer can include wanting the wrongdoing to stop.**

Compassion is therefore not equivalent to approval.

### 4. MN 61 — accountability aims at recognition and future restraint

MN 61 repeatedly teaches examination of action before, during, and after.

When a harmful bodily or verbal act has been done, the response includes:
- acknowledging/disclosing the act;
- clarifying it with teacher or wise companions;
- restraining oneself in the future.

The direction is corrective and prospective:
see the harm accurately and reduce recurrence.

A167 uses this as an early-discourse foundation for accountability without self-punitive fixation.

### 5. AN 5.167 — accusation itself is ethically constrained

AN 5.167 states that one who accuses another should establish five qualities:
- right time;
- factuality;
- gentleness;
- relevance to the goal/matter;
- goodwill rather than inner aversion.

The person being accused is instructed to remain grounded in truth and unprovokedness.

This is direct evidence that even **calling someone to account** has ethical constraints.

### 6. MN 65 — disciplinary pressure can vary according to the offender's response

MN 65 discusses cases in which repeated pressure or investigation is applied differently depending on patterns such as:
- frequent offenses;
- evasiveness;
- anger/bitterness when corrected;
- failure to behave properly or mend one's ways;
- versus greater responsiveness to correction.

A167 draws only the bounded claim:

**early monastic material recognizes proportionate, differentiated disciplinary response rather than a single undifferentiated reaction to wrongdoing.**

Guardrail:
MN 65 is a monastic disciplinary context. It is not a universal modern criminal-justice code.

### 7. Prior A160 guardrail — relinquishing resentment does not compel restored trust

A160 already established from early material that:
- non-hatred;
- forgiveness;
- reconciliation;
- restored trust;
- restored access

must not be collapsed.

A167 therefore carries forward:
one may remove hatred while maintaining consequences, separation, or protective boundaries.

## CROSS_TEXT_SYNTHESIS

### A. Five functions of consequence should be separated

A167 distinguishes possible functions of consequence:

1. **protection** — stop or reduce further harm;
2. **accountability** — name conduct accurately and connect action with responsibility;
3. **instruction/correction** — help the offender understand and change;
4. **boundary/community integrity** — preserve conditions needed for safety or practice;
5. **revenge** — inflict suffering because one wants the offender to suffer in return.

The first four can be compatible with non-hatred.
The fifth is in direct tension with the non-hostility direction of Dhammapada 3–5 and resentment-removal texts.

This fivefold map is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS, not a canonical list.

### B. Consequence and hatred are separable

A167's central synthesis is:

**The necessity of a consequence is one question; the mental wish to make the offender suffer is another.**

A boundary can be strict while the mind is not vindictive.

A sanction can be necessary while humiliation is unnecessary.

### C. Compassion may support restraint

If someone's conduct is destructive to self and others, compassion need not mean letting that conduct continue.

AN 5.162 gives a direct template:
the wish is that the person abandon wrong conduct and develop right conduct.

Thus, in synthesis:
sometimes restraint or exclusion may serve protection and correction better than permissiveness.

### D. Proportionality is a guarded inference, not a fully developed theory

MN 65 supports differentiated disciplinary response according to conduct and responsiveness.

A167 therefore allows a limited synthesis:
**responses should track relevant facts, risk, repetition, acknowledgment, and prospects for reform rather than anger alone.**

It does **not** claim that the Nikāyas supply a complete modern doctrine of proportional punishment.

### E. Rehabilitation and protection can diverge

A person may deserve compassion and opportunity to change while still being unsafe to trust.

Therefore:
- wishing for reform does not require immediate restoration of access;
- observing reform over time may matter;
- protection of others can remain necessary even while hatred is abandoned.

This carries forward A160 and is CROSS_TEXT SYNTHESIS.

### F. "They must suffer because I suffered" is not the same as accountability

A167 distinguishes:
- **accountability:** "this action caused harm; it must stop; responsibility and suitable consequence follow";
- **revenge:** "I want your suffering to answer my suffering."

The early texts strongly support abandoning the second mental movement.

The exact modern wording is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION.

## LATER/PARACANONICAL — MILINDAPAÑHA

### "Kindness and Punishment"

The later dialogue explicitly wrestles with the apparent conflict between kindness/non-harm and punishment/correction.

A167 uses only the narrow support:
- kindness does not require absence of all consequence;
- correction may be framed as connected to conduct rather than personal hatred.

Guardrail:
the passage includes severe punitive material that A167 does **not** promote into an early Buddhist rule.

### "Dismissal of the Elders"

The later text presents dismissal/correction as compatible with absence of anger.

Role:
supports the distinction between:
- exclusion as a functional response;
- exclusion as hatred or revenge.

### Mil 7.5.5 — mongoose simile

The simile of entering a conflictual world protected by love remains useful:
one can confront danger or conflict while guarding the mind from enmity.

## UNCERTAINTY / OPEN CHECK

1. "Justice", "punishment", "rehabilitation", and "proportionality" are modern analytic terms, not assumed to map one-to-one onto a single early Pāli vocabulary.
2. MN 65 and AN 5.167 operate in monastic disciplinary settings; extension to state criminal justice is not text-attested here.
3. A167 does not claim that all punishment is Buddhist or that every protective measure is automatically skillful.
4. A consequence imposed with claimed "compassion" can still conceal anger, domination, group interest, or cruelty; motive must not be inferred from self-description alone.
5. Non-hatred does not remove the need for factual investigation or due process.
6. Milindapañha punitive details remain later/paracanonical and cannot override early-discourse standards.
7. Vinaya-level disciplinary procedure deserves a separate audit before stronger institutional conclusions are made.

## Practical understanding

When deciding a consequence after harm, ask:

1. What harm actually occurred?
2. What needs to be stopped or protected now?
3. What responsibility is factually attributable?
4. What consequence serves protection, correction, or community integrity?
5. Is the response stronger because risk/repetition is greater, or because I am angrier?
6. Am I trying to reduce future harm, or make the offender suffer in return?
7. Can I maintain the consequence without contempt?
8. What evidence would justify reducing the restriction later?
9. What evidence would justify keeping or strengthening it?

This is CROSS_TEXT APPLICATION, not a canonical nine-step list.
